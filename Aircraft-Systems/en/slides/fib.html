Optic fiber

A Vietnamese and English transcript of each slide, read directly from the image (37/37 slides). Module using this deck: M08. Where a slide prints something wrong or inconsistent it is listed under "Slide errors"; the transcript keeps what the slide prints.

Slide 1: Fiber optics

Title slide "Fiber optics" with two photos captioned "Example of Optical fibers": a green fibre patch cord with connectors and a bundle of coloured glowing fibres.

Slide 2: OUTLINE

Outline: What are optical fibers? Structure of optical fibers. Working principle of optical fibers. Optical fiber communication systems. Conclusion. References. MCQ.

Slide 3: What are Optical Fibers?

An optical fibre is a waveguide along which an electromagnetic wave (light) can pass with very little loss. Structure: core (higher refractive index n1), cladding (lower index n2 < n1), protective coating (mechanical protection); a cable has many fibres, strength members (for example aramid yarn) and an outer jacket. Light from an LED or laser is kept inside the core by total internal reflection and reaches a photodetector. Example communication system: electrical input signal, light source (LED or laser), optical fibre, photodetector (for example a photodiode), electrical output signal.

Slide 4: History of Optical Fiber Technology

Timeline. 1910: dielectric waveguide analysis (Hondros and Debye), showed that light can be guided in a cylindrical dielectric structure by total internal reflection. 1970: first low-loss optical fibre (Corning), attenuation below 20 dB/km, enabling long-distance optical communication (graph of attenuation against year with a 20 dB/km line). 1990: first use of optical fibre in aircraft, for high-speed data transmission, immune to electromagnetic interference and lighter. 2020: still an ongoing process for aircraft, widely used and improved in modern aircraft. Intervals shown: about 60 years, 20 years, 30 years.

Numbers: Corning 1970: under 20 dB/km

Slide 5: Today: Growing use of optical fiber networks in aircraft

The growth of bandwidth-hungry applications leads to more optical fibre networks in the cabin and on the flight deck. In the cockpit: electronic flight bags (EFB), head-up display (HUD), large flight deck displays, cameras (for example enhanced vision) and other avionics systems joined by an avionics network switch over optical fibre. In the passenger cabin: on-demand movies and TV, internet access (Wi-Fi), games and shopping on seat displays, through a cabin network switch and a Wi-Fi access point linked by optical fibre.

Slide 6: Aircraft using standardised optical fibers

A grid of twelve aircraft: A350-XWB, A380-800, Boeing 787, Airbus A340-600 (airliners); Boeing-Lockheed Martin F-22 Lightning, BAE Systems Typhoon, Airbus A400M, LMCO F-35 (military); AW-101 Merlin HC.3, Boeing Chinook HC.3 (labelled "Unmanned aircraft / Concept"), Boeing Chinook HC.3 (labelled "Helicopter"), AW-159 Wildcat (helicopters). Why standardised optical fibres: high bandwidth, light weight compared with copper, immunity to EMI, standardised fibres and connectors for reliability, interoperability and easier maintenance. Typical applications on an aircraft diagram: passenger cabin (IFE, Wi-Fi, seat control, lighting, cabin monitoring), flight deck (EFB, enhanced vision, video-rich displays, avionics networks), aircraft network backbone (optical switches, high-speed data buses), flight control and sensors, engines and wings (health monitoring, sensors and control, high-temperature tolerant fibre), tail section (communication systems, antennas, data links).

Slide 7: Tomorrow: Wider use of optical fiber networks in aircraft

Optical networks are already built into new aircraft such as the Airbus A380 and Boeing 787; many older aircraft need wire and cable upgrades for higher bandwidth, especially for IFE: traditional copper wire is heavier, has limited bandwidth and needs upgrading; fibre networks give higher bandwidth, lighter weight and readiness for future applications. Next applications: 1 data communications (avionics, IFE and aircraft systems); 2 secure communications (low probability of interception); 3 fibre-optic sensing: optical fibres as sensors for strain (skin and structure), temperature (engines, environment) and impact detection (composites). Examples: impact detection (location and severity, useful for composites), strain measurement (loads on wings and fuselage, structural health monitoring), temperature measurement (high temperatures in engines, real-time monitoring).

Slide 8: Light: an electromagnetic wave

Light is an electromagnetic wave; its spatial periodicity is the wavelength (lambda), its time periodicity the period T = 1/f. Relationship: lambda = v/f = vT (lambda wavelength in m, v propagation speed in m/s, f frequency in Hz, T period in s). Electromagnetic spectrum from gamma rays to radio waves; visible light about 400 to 700 nm; infrared lies between visible light and microwaves. Speed of light in vacuum c about 3 x 10^8 m/s. Speed in a non-magnetic dielectric: v = c / sqrt(eps_r), light travels slower in a material because of the higher refractive index (eps_r relative permittivity). Example for silica: n about 1.44 (eps_r = n^2 about 2.07), v = c/n = 3 x 10^8 / 1.44 about 2.08 x 10^8 m/s.

Formulas: lambda = v/f = vT · v = c / sqrt(eps_r) · v = c/n = 3 x 10^8 / 1,44 = 2,08 x 10^8 m/s

Numbers: c = 3 x 10^8 m/s; visible light 400 to 700 nm; silica n = 1,44

Checked by computation: 3 x 10^8 / 1.44 = 2.083 x 10^8 m/s and 1.44^2 = 2.07. Correct.

Slide 9: Refractive index

The speed of light depends on the material; the refractive index n tells how much light is slowed compared with vacuum. n = sqrt(eps_r); v (m/s) = c (m/s) / n; c about 3.0 x 10^8 m/s. In air or vacuum eps_r = 1 so n = 1 and v = c. A ray crosses from air (n1 about 1) into glass (n2 about 1.5) bending toward the normal (theta1, theta2 drawn). Table (material, relative permittivity, refractive index, speed of light v): vacuum 1.0, 1.00, 3.00 x 10^8; air (1 atm) 1.0006, 1.0003 (about 1), about 3.00 x 10^8; water about 80, about 1.33, about 2.25 x 10^8; glass (typical) about 2.25, about 1.50, about 2.00 x 10^8; acrylic (PMMA) about 2.56, about 1.60, about 1.87 x 10^8.

Formulas: n = sqrt(eps_r) · v = c/n

Numbers: water eps_r about 80, n about 1.33; glass n about 1.50, v about 2.00 x 10^8 m/s

Slide 10: Structure of an optical fiber

A cutaway of a fibre: outer jacket (plastic, mechanical protection), coating (silicone rubber, protects the fibre, typical diameter 250 to 300 um), cladding (lower refractive index n2, keeps light in the core by total internal reflection) and core (transparent dielectric of higher index n1 carrying the light, typical diameter 5 to 100 um). Typical dimensions: core diameter 5 to 100 um (for example 9 and 50 um), coating diameter 250 to 300 um, outer jacket diameter about 0.9 to 3 mm. Also: cross-section, light propagation by total internal reflection, and the step refractive index profile (core n1 > cladding n2).

Numbers: core 5 to 100 um (examples 9, 50 um); cladding 250 to 300 um; outer jacket about 0.9 to 3 mm

Slide 11: Working principle of an optical fiber

Light is guided in the core by repeated total internal reflections (TIR) at the core-cladding interface until it emerges at the other end, even if the fibre is bent. TIR occurs when the incidence angle exceeds the critical angle: theta_i > theta_c = arcsin(n2/n1), with n1 > n2. Key points: the core has a higher refractive index than the cladding; light is confined in the core by TIR; the ray can propagate long distances and still be guided even when the fibre is bent.

Formulas: theta_i > theta_c = arcsin(n2/n1), n1 > n2

Slide 12: Refraction at the interface and Snell's law

Refraction at the interface between two dielectric media: incident angle theta_i and refracted angle theta_r measured from the normal. Snell's law n1 sin(theta_i) = n2 sin(theta_r). Case 1: n2 > n1 gives theta_r < theta_i, the ray bends toward the normal. Case 2: n2 < n1 gives theta_r > theta_i, the ray bends away from the normal. Text: when light enters a medium of higher refractive index it bends toward the normal; of lower refractive index it bends away from the normal.

Formulas: n1 sin(theta_i) = n2 sin(theta_r)

Slide errors and doubts
  • This slide has the ray-bending rule right; Tooley section 10.2 writes this rule backwards (recorded in Module 08).

Slide 13: Ray theory transmission and critical angle

If n1 > n2 the angle of refraction is always greater than the angle of incidence. When the angle of refraction is 90 degrees the ray emerges parallel to the interface; this limiting case defines the critical angle theta_c: sin(theta_c) = n2/n1, defined for n1 > n2; for theta_i = theta_c the refracted ray travels along the interface; for theta_i > theta_c there is total internal reflection and no transmission. Three sketches: refraction from high to low index (theta_r > theta_i), the critical angle (theta_r = 90 degrees), total internal reflection (theta_i > theta_c).

Formulas: sin(theta_c) = n2/n1

Slide 14: Total internal reflection (TIR)

Derivation of the critical angle from Snell's law with theta_r = 90 degrees and sin 90 = 1, so sin(theta_c) = n2/n1 for n1 > n2 (example medium 1 glass n1 about 1.5, medium 2 air n2 about 1.0). At the critical angle the refracted ray travels along the boundary; for theta_i > theta_c no refraction occurs and the light is completely reflected back (TIR). Three cases for n1 > n2: theta_i < theta_c (refraction and partial reflection), theta_i = theta_c (refracted ray along the boundary), theta_i > theta_c (all the light reflected). Application: light is confined in the core by repeated TIR at the core-cladding interface.

Formulas: sin(theta_c) = n2/n1

Numbers: glass n1 about 1.5; air n2 about 1.0

Slide 15: Propagation of light through an optical fiber

A light wave in the core that meets the core-cladding interface at an angle of incidence greater than or equal to the critical angle is totally internally reflected, so it is guided along the core by a series of total internal reflections (TIR). Condition for TIR: light travels from a higher index to a lower index (n1 > n2); if theta_i >= theta_c the refracted ray cannot escape into the cladding; sin(theta_c) = n2/n1 with n1 the core index and n2 the cladding index. Figures: a straight and a bent fibre with the ray bouncing at the core-cladding interface, and a zoom at the interface with incident ray, refracted ray (in cladding) and the reflected ray label.

Formulas: sin(theta_c) = n2/n1

Slide errors and doubts

Slide 16: Acceptance angle of an optical fiber

Only rays entering within the acceptance angle theta_a are coupled into the fibre and guided; the cone of acceptance has half-angle theta_a. In air (n0 about 1): sin(theta_a) = NA = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2). General form with incident medium index n0: n0 sin(theta_a) = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2). Relation with the critical angle: theta_i >= theta_c gives total internal reflection, sin(theta_c) = n2/n1 (n1 > n2); the acceptance angle is the maximum external incident angle for which the ray, once inside the core, meets the core-cladding interface at or above theta_c. A cross-section shows outer jacket (plastic), cladding (lower n2) and core (higher n1).

Formulas: sin(theta_a) = NA = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2) · n0 sin(theta_a) = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2)

Slide 17: Acceptance angle and numerical aperture

Only rays entering within the acceptance angle theta_a can be guided by TIR. Refraction at the air-core interface (point A): n0 sin(theta_1) = n1 sin(theta_2), with theta_1 the incident angle to the fibre axis. TIR condition at the core-cladding interface: sin(theta_c) = n2/n1 (n1 > n2). Acceptance angle and numerical aperture: sin(theta_a) = NA = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2)/n0; for light from air (n0 about 1) NA = sin(theta_a) = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2). Three small panels: incident ray within acceptance angle, ray propagation in the core, and the NA relations.

Formulas: n0 sin(theta_1) = n1 sin(theta_2) · NA = sin(theta_a) = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2)

Slide 18: Ray geometry inside the fiber (triangle ABC)

A ray in air (n0) enters the core at A with external angle theta_1, refracts to theta_2 and strikes the core-cladding interface at B with angle phi from the normal. From the right triangle ABC: theta_2 = pi/2 - phi, where phi must be at least the critical angle theta_c at the core-cladding interface for TIR. Geometric relation at the reflection point: sin(pi/2 - phi) = cos(phi), so sin(theta_2) = cos(phi). Snell at the input: n0 sin(theta_1) = n1 sin(theta_2) = n1 cos(phi). Symbol list: n0 incident medium (air about 1), n1 core, n2 cladding, theta_1 external incidence angle, phi angle at the core-cladding interface (phi >= theta_c).

Formulas: theta_2 = pi/2 - phi · n0 sin(theta_1) = n1 cos(phi)

Slide 19: Acceptance angle calculation and numerical aperture

Seven panels. 1 Fibre geometry and ray propagation. 2 Refraction at the air-core interface: n0 sin(theta_a) = n1 sin(theta_1). 3 Critical angle at the core-cladding interface: sin(theta_c) = n2/n1 (n1 > n2). 4 Relationship between acceptance angle and critical angle: in the limiting case for TIR the internal angle at the first reflection equals the critical angle: theta_1 = theta_c = theta_a. 5 Numerical aperture: combining the two limiting cases NA = n0 sin(theta_a) = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2); NA represents the light-gathering capability; only rays with theta_1 <= theta_a are guided. 6 In air (n0 = 1): NA = sin(theta_a). 7 Relative refractive index difference Delta = (n1^2 - n2^2)/(2 n1^2); for small Delta, NA is approximated as n1 (2 Delta)^(1/2).

Formulas: NA = n0 sin(theta_a) = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2) · Delta = (n1^2 - n2^2)/(2 n1^2) · NA = n1 (2 Delta)^(1/2)

Slide 21: Modes of propagation in an optical fiber

Five panels. 1 The numerical aperture characterises the ability of the fibre to focus the light wave in the core; light propagates as many separate rays, each following a different path called a mode; each mode satisfies the TIR condition. 2 Ray representation: high-order mode (large angle), medium-order, low-order mode (small angle). 3 Mode order: a small propagation angle (near the fibre axis) gives a weak (low) order; an angle near the critical angle gives a strong (high) order; higher-order modes travel at larger angles with longer paths, causing higher modal dispersion. 4 NA = sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2); normalised frequency (V number) V = (pi d / lambda) sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2) = (pi d / lambda) NA, with d core diameter, lambda wavelength. 5 Number of modes in a multimode step-index fibre for large V (V >> 1): N about V^2 / 2.

Formulas: V = (pi d / lambda) sqrt(n1^2 - n2^2) = (pi d / lambda) NA · N = V^2 / 2

Numbers: N is about V^2/2 (V >> 1)

Slide 23: Transmit information through optical fibers

An optical communication system has an optical transmitter, an optical fibre with connectors and an optical receiver. Logic signal 1 0 1 0 1 in; transmitter (LED or laser diode) converts electrical signals to light, typically 850 nm, 1310 nm or 1550 nm; connectors couple light into and out of the fibre and minimise coupling loss and alignment error; the fibre has a high-index core (n1) and a low-index cladding (n2 < n1), guides light by total internal reflection and allows low-loss long-distance transmission; the receiver (photodiode, PIN or APD, followed by an amplifier and signal processing circuit) converts light back to electrical signals and the logic output 1 0 1 0 1 appears.

Numbers: 850 nm, 1310 nm, 1550 nm

Slide 24: Propagation limitations in optical fibers

Three limitations. 1 Attenuation: the optical power becomes smaller with distance due to absorption, scattering and bending losses; graph of optical power from 0 to -30 dB over 0 to 100 km with the note attenuation about 0.2 to 0.5 dB/km at 1550 nm; main causes: material absorption, Rayleigh scattering, bending. 2 Temporal (modal) dispersion: different propagation angles (modes) have different path lengths, causing a delay between them and a broadened output pulse; high-order modes (longer path) arrive later than low-order modes. 3 Chromatic dispersion: different wavelengths travel at different group velocities in the fibre material, causing delay and broadening; graph: refractive index n decreases with increasing wavelength (normal dispersion) between 1300 and 1600 nm.

Numbers: 0.2 to 0.5 dB/km at 1550 nm (as on the slide)

Slide 25: Attenuation in optical fibers

The optical power decreases with distance due to absorption, scattering and other losses. 1 Attenuation equation (exponential form): P(z) = P_in e^(-alpha z), with alpha the attenuation coefficient in Np/m and z the distance. 2 Attenuation in dB/km: A = -(1/L) 10 log10 [P(L)/P(0)], with A in dB/km, P(0) input power, P(L) output power at distance L (km). 3 Graph of optical power along the fibre decaying from P_in to P(L). 4 Effect on pulses: attenuation reduces the pulse amplitude and different wavelength components travelling at slightly different speeds broaden the pulse (temporal dispersion).

Formulas: P(z) = P_in e^(-alpha z) · A = -(1/L) 10 log10 [P(L)/P(0)] (dB/km)

Checked by computation: the conversion factor from dB/km to Np/km is 4.343 (10/ln 10); for example 0.35 dB/km corresponds to alpha = 0.0806 Np/km.

Graph of attenuation coefficient alpha (dB/km, 0.01 to 10, log scale) against wavelength (0.6 to 2.0 um) with three regions. Slide 28: Modal dispersion

In a multimode fibre rays propagate at different angles (modes); each ray has a different path length and reaches the end at a different time; rays at larger angles (blue) take a longer path and arrive later than rays near the axis (green); the result is a spread of the output pulse. 1 Path length of a ray at angle theta: L(theta) = L / cos(theta). 2 Transit time difference, with group velocity in the core v_g about c/n1: Delta t = Delta L / v_g about ((n1 - n2) / c) L, where n1 and n2 are the core and cladding indices (n1 > n2); typically Delta t / L about 10 to 50 ns/km. 3 Pulse spreading: two input pulses arrive as three groups (axial green first, intermediate red, high-angle blue last) and merge into two broadened pulses.

Formulas: L(theta) = L / cos(theta) · Delta t = Delta L / v_g = ((n1 - n2) / c) L

  • Numeric mismatch: with n1 - n2 = 0.02 to 0.03 as in the slide 20 exercise, the formula gives about 67 to 100 ns/km (checked in Python: Delta n / c x 1 km = 33 ns/km for Delta n = 0.01), outside the "10 to 50 ns/km" written on the slide.

Slide 29: Chromatic dispersion in optical fiber

The refractive index depends on wavelength (material dispersion), n = n(lambda), so v(lambda) = c / n(lambda) and the different wavelengths in a pulse travel at different velocities, which broadens the pulse. Pulse broadening: Delta t(lambda0) = L D(lambda0) Delta lambda. D(lambda) is the chromatic dispersion parameter: D(lambda) = (1/L) d t_g / d lambda = -(1/c)(d n / d lambda) L, in ps/(nm km); it can be computed with the Sellmeier equation; at the zero-dispersion wavelength lambda0, D(lambda0) = 0. Panels: pulse broadening concept (spectrum width Delta lambda around lambda0), n against wavelength with dn/dlambda < 0 between 1200 and 1600 nm, and the effect on the output pulse (shorter wavelength higher n lower v, centre wavelength lambda0, longer wavelength lower n higher v).

Formulas: Delta t(lambda0) = L D(lambda0) Delta lambda · D(lambda) = (1/L) d t_g / d lambda = -(1/c)(d n / d lambda) L

Slide errors and doubts
  • Dimensional inconsistency: D has been divided by L (units ps/(nm km)) but the right side -(1/c)(dn/dlambda) L still contains L. The standard form of the material dispersion parameter is D = -(lambda/c) d^2 n / d lambda^2; the formula on the slide should not be used for calculation.

Slide 30: Types of optical fibers

Three types. 1 Multimode step-index (MM-SI): uniform core index with a step at the cladding boundary, many modes with different angles and path lengths, large core (about 50 to 200 um), high modal dispersion so lower bandwidth. 2 Multimode graded-index (the panel title reads "MM-SI"): the core index decreases gradually from centre to edge (usually parabolic); higher-order modes travel faster in lower-index regions, reducing differential delay; smaller core (about 50 to 100 um); lower modal dispersion than step-index, so higher bandwidth. 3 Single-mode step-index (SM-SI): small core (about 8 to 10 um), one propagation mode (nearly axial ray), no modal dispersion, highest bandwidth, used for long-distance high-speed communication. Comparison table: core diameter 50 to 200 um (MM-SI), 50 to 100 um (MM-GI), 8 to 10 um (SM-SI); index profile step, graded (parabolic), step; modes many (hundreds), many (tens to hundreds), one; modal dispersion high, lower, none; bandwidth low, higher, highest; applications LANs and data centres, campus networks, telecom and backbone.

  • Does not match Tooley/Module 08: the slide gives a single-mode core of about 8 to 10 um and multimode 50 to 200 um; Tooley Figure 10.5 uses 5/125 um for single mode and 50/125 um for multimode (two different ways of stating the core size).

Slide 31: Fibre optic cable

The construction of a typical fibre optic cable: five optical fibres and two filler strands, separator tape, aramid yarn strength member, an outer jacket. The buffers are coded with colours (blue, red, green, yellow, white) to identify the fibres. Components: outer jacket (polyethylene PE or PVC, protects from mechanical damage, moisture, chemicals and environment); aramid yarn strength member (for example Kevlar, tensile strength); polyester separator tape (keeps fibres organised); buffer (polymer coating typically 250 um diameter, identified by colour); optical fibre (core about 8 to 62.5 um transmits light, cladding of lower index about 125 um); filler strands (non-optical elements such as plastic rods that keep the cable shape). Colour coding example: blue fibre 1, red fibre 2, green fibre 3, yellow fibre 4, white fibre 5.

Numbers: 5 optical fibres + 2 filler fibres; buffer 250 um; reflective cladding 125 um

Slide 33: MCQ 1 to 4

Slide 36: More exercises (number of modes and core diameter)

A step-index multimode fibre with NA 0.20 supports approximately 1000 modes at 850 nm. What is the diameter of its core? How many modes does the fibre support at 1320 nm and 1550 nm? Formulas: V = 2 pi a (NA) / lambda; a = V lambda / 2 pi (NA); M = V^2 / 2; V = sqrt(2M); d = 2a. Printed answer: d = 60.49 um; at 1320 nm V = 28.78, M = 414; at 1550 nm V = 24.51, M = 301.

Formulas: V = 2 pi a NA / lambda · M = V^2 / 2 · d = 2a

Numbers: d = 60,49 um; 1320 nm: V = 28,78, M = 414; 1550 nm: V = 24,51, M = 301

Checked by computation: V(850 nm) = sqrt(2000) = 44.72; a = 30.25 um so d = 60.50 um; at 1320 nm V = 28.80 and M = 415; at 1550 nm V = 24.52 and M = 301. The printed answers agree within rounding (V differs by 0.02 and M by 1 at 1320 nm).
