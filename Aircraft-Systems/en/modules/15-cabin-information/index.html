15. Cabin systems and aircraft information systems

The systems that serve passengers and information on a modern aircraft: the cabin network server (ARINC 628), IFE, announcements, interphone and chimes, then the electronic flight bag (EFB), integrated information systems and the lessons of the G-EZWE case. Built from Tooley Chapters 19 and 20 (no lecturer slides).

PART 1/3
01

Cabin systems put entertainment, announcements, environment and communications on one network server to cut cabling and weight

  • Cabin functions and the need for integration
  • The cabin network server (CNSS) and ARINC 628
  • The IFE subsystem: head-end, distribution, seats

Cabin services used to be separate cable-heavy systems and are now gathered on a shared network

(Tooley 19.1.) The cabin offers safety briefings, flight-crew announcements, in-flight entertainment (IFE), passenger address (ceiling loudspeakers and IFE), telephone, internet and email, alerting and calling, zone environmental control and e-commerce. On older aircraft each service had its own cabling, power, controls and displays, heavy and power-hungry: for instance a PA system with several 60 to 120 W amplifiers feeding zoned ceiling loudspeakers over very long cables, and IFE with several disk drives and cabling to every seat. Moving to a network system integrates analogue and digital systems, allows flexible reconfiguration, raises safety, meets passenger expectations and reduces weight and fuel.

📖 Explanation for beginners

Compare IMA (Module 13): the same idea of replacing many separate systems with one shared platform on a network, but cabin services are not flight-critical.

The cabin network server system (CNSS) lets audio, video and data share one network resource, following ARINC 628

(Tooley 19.2.) Four typical subsystems: IFE, cabin environmental control, passenger address (PA) and communications; network and processing resources are shared. Four kinds of user: the flight crew (from the flight deck), cabin crew (tablets), passengers (seat units, personal devices) and engineering staff (laptops during maintenance, a media loader to update media and configuration data). ARINC 628 defines the general architectural philosophy and infrastructure: commercial (COTS) servers and high-speed wired and wireless communication; it specifies no individual equipment, so manufacturers and operators are very flexible.

📖 Explanation for beginners

ARINC 628 is the standard for the cabin information network; do not confuse it with ARINC 429 (a low-speed bus), ARINC 629 and ARINC 664/AFDX (Modules 05 and 13). This is exactly Chapter 19 Q2.

An IFE has a head-end in the avionics bay, cabin distribution and a seat subsystem; media loading needs a fast link

(Tooley 19.3.) Head-end: a system controller (SC) with media storage, an interface to other systems and multiplexers/distribution units for audio and video. Cabin distribution: cabling, wall- and ceiling-mounted tapping boxes and area distribution units routing video, audio and data to seats and screens. Seat: a seat electronic box (SEB) and a passenger control unit (PCU). The control panel (CP) is in the cabin crew area, often near the forward left cabin door with the data load ports. In-seat telephones connect through the IFE network to the SC and then via SATCOM. A media loader fills the SC's mass memory: 100 Mbps may suffice but Gigabit Ethernet is preferred.

File loading time
$$t=\frac{8\times\text{size}}{\text{rate}}\qquad(4.7\text{ GB}:\;t=376\text{ s at }100\text{ Mbit/s},\;37.6\text{ s at }1\text{ Gbit/s})$$
📖 Explanation for beginners

The 4.7 GB size and the 1 GB = 10⁹ bytes convention are the site's assumptions for illustration, not numbers from the book; the calculation ignores protocol overhead.

PART 2/3
02

Cabin crew run announcements, interphone, IFE and environment from the FAP; announcements always outrank IFE and chimes follow a tone convention

  • Attendant panels, announcements and interphone
  • The 494 Hz and 587 Hz chimes

The forward attendant panel controls announcements, interphone, IFE configuration and the environment, with five announcement priority levels

(Tooley 19.3.1 to 19.3.4.) The FAP is usually near the forward left door and more complex than the other panels: passenger announcements, interphone communication, control of IFE services, environmental monitoring and control. Announcements go through cabin loudspeakers and IFE headsets, with priority levels programmed in the cabin configuration data:

LevelSource of the announcement
1flight deck
2forward attendant panel (FAP)
3rear or other attendant panels
4pre-recorded announcements
5boarding music and IFE

The crew also choose audio and video channels, video on demand (VOD), flight information and moving maps, telephone, games and internet; passengers can always adjust their reading lights and local airflow, while the cabin crew control zone lighting at every stage of flight.

📖 Explanation for beginners

A service interphone lets the flight deck, cabin crew and ground crew talk while the aircraft is on the ground, through a jack at the ground electrical panel. While the IFE is on, announcements always take priority.

Cabin chimes are 900 ms sine bursts at 494 Hz (low) and 587 Hz (high), combined into four signals through the PA system

(Tooley 19.3.5.) Crew alerting has several audible and visual forms to cope with cases such as "ready for take-off/landing" or "do not disturb". The low and high chimes are pure sine bursts of 900 ms at 494 Hz and 587 Hz:

SignalToneSent from, to
Passenger calla single high toneseats and toilets to the cabin crew
Attendant callhigh/lowa crew station to other crew stations
Attendant emergency callhigh/low repeated three timesflight crew to cabin crew
Seat belt sign illuminateda single low tonetells that the seat belt sign is on
Cycles and period of the chimes
$$N=f\,t=494\times0.9\approx445\text{ cycles},\qquad T=\frac1f=2.02\text{ ms}\;(494\text{ Hz}),\;1.70\text{ ms}\;(587\text{ Hz})$$
📖 Explanation for beginners

The ratio 587/494 = 1.188 is close to a minor third (1.2). The chimes are played through the PA system and mean something only to crew members. The site computed the cycle count and period two ways (multiplication and rational division): they agree.

PART 3/3
03

The EFB replaces the pilot's flight bag with electronics, in portable and installed classes and Type A and B applications; the G-EZWE case shows a data error can be dangerous

  • Aircraft information systems and the take-off calculation
  • EFB: equipment classes and software types
  • The G-EZWE case and integrated information systems

An information system is people, software, hardware and data; the take-off calculation needs V1, VR, V2 and the available distance

(Tooley 20.1.) An information system is a computer system with software that collects, stores and processes data to give information and knowledge; people are part of it. Four kinds on a transport aircraft: EFB and electronic logbooks (ELB), cabin information systems, maintenance information systems and air traffic and information management systems (ATIMS). Take-off example: the take-off distance is the ground run plus the horizontal distance to climb to a 35 ft screen height; it depends on weight, air density, wind, runway condition and flap configuration.

SpeedMeaning
V1the maximum speed at which the pilot must begin to stop after a critical engine failure
VRthe speed at which the nose gear is raised
VLOthe lift-off speed
V2the safety speed at the screen height
VSthe stalling speed

Boeing 737 example (79,000 kg, TODA 2.1 km, dry): V1 = 124 kt, VR = 125 kt, V2 = 136 kt.

📖 Explanation for beginners

The three speeds of the example increase 124 < 125 < 136 kt, in the order V1, VR, V2. The figures were checked on page image 320 of the book.

EFBs split into portable (no certification) and installed (part of the type design); Type A software has no safety effect, Type B needs assessment

(Tooley 20.2.) The EFB replaces the pilot's flight bag: electronic documents, performance calculation, charts and maps, taxiway position awareness, video surveillance and links to company systems. An EFB must not replace required installed equipment. Before 2017 there were Class 1 (non-installed COTS), Class 2 (modified COTS, possibly needing a supplemental certificate) and Class 3 (installed, subject to airworthiness requirements); now there are two classes:

📖 Explanation for beginners

An application that actively controls the aircraft or duplicates a certified avionics system is not an EFB application and needs full airworthiness approval (Chapter 20 Q3).

G-EZWE: the wrong runway length in the EFB made the aircraft lift off with only 110 m left; an integrated information system separates the "avionics world" from the "open world" with a firewall

(Tooley 20.1.1, 20.2.5, 20.3.) In September 2019 an Airbus A320-214 (G-EZWE) had a serious incident at Lisbon. The AAIB attributed it to incorrect data in the take-off performance calculation: the crew believed they had selected the shortest runway length but used the full length while taking off from a taxiway intersection; the distance available was 1,395 m less than used and the aircraft lifted off with only 110 m of runway left. The calculation application was in the EFB. Lessons: validate EFB data, cross-check calculations before entering them in the flight management computer, reduce distractions; the operator moved to a version with a picture of the runway. That day the captain's EFB cradle was broken and the battery was weak. In an integrated information system (20.3) a network server (NSS) hosts on-board information applications for three domains (avionics, flight operations, communication/cabin) with four kinds of user interface; Boeing's AIMS is IMA-based.

📖 Explanation for beginners

This shows the consequence of a data-entry error in an information system: correct software with wrong input data is still dangerous. The figures (1,395 m, 110 m) are quoted from the book.
