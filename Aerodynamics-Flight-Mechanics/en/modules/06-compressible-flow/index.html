MODULE 06 · Compressible flow

Compressible flow: isentropic relations and normal shock waves

Module 03 only touched on Mach number, critical Mach number and shock waves qualitatively, with a few numbers. This module lays the quantitative foundation: the calorically perfect gas, stagnation quantities (p₀, T₀), isentropic relations in terms of Mach number, then the normal-shock relations and what the loss of total pressure means. The Anderson ch. 7 and 8 problems are recomputed and compared with the Solution Manual. Three of them (8.8, 8.9, 8.16) give round Mach numbers (3.0, 2.5, 2.9), which makes them easy to self-check.

Anderson ch. 7 (Problems 7.1, 7.4, 7.5) and ch. 8 (Problems 8.3, 8.5, 8.8, 8.9, 8.16) · EASA Module 08, Sub-module 03 (pp. 3.10–3.13)

PART 1/5
01

The calorically perfect gas

  • p = ρRT with constant c_p, c_v and γ = c_p/c_v = 1.4 for air
  • Speed of sound a = √(γRT); Mach number M = V/a
  • Reversible adiabatic flow keeps the entropy constant
PART 1/5 · 06

Equation of state at a stagnation point (Anderson 7.1)

  • Missile nose: T₀ = 934 °R and p₀ = 7.8 atm = 16 505 lb/ft², so ρ₀ = p/(RT) = 0.01030 slug/ft³ (R = 1716 ft·lb/(slug·°R))
  • Anderson 8.1 already gave a = 304.0 m/s at 230 K (γ = 1.4, R = 287)
  • The gas constant R = 287 J/(kg·K) = 1716 ft·lb/(slug·°R) for air
In plain words
Always use absolute temperature (K or °R) and absolute pressure in p = ρRT.
PART 2/5
02

Total quantities and isentropic relations

  • T₀/T = 1 + (γ−1)M²/2; p₀/p = (T₀/T)^{γ/(γ−1)}; ρ₀/ρ = (T₀/T)^{1/(γ−1)}
  • All total quantities are constant along a reversible adiabatic flow
  • From the Mach number and p, T at a point we get p₀, T₀

PART 2/5 · 06

Isentropic flow over an airfoil and in a nozzle (Anderson 7.4, 7.5)

  • 7.4: T∞ = 245 K, p∞ = 4.35×10⁴, p = 3.6×10⁴ N/m² gives ρ = ρ∞(p/p∞)^{1/γ} = 0.5404 kg/m³
  • 7.5: T₀ = 500 K, p₀ = 10 atm, p_e = 1 atm gives T_e = T₀(p_e/p₀)^{(γ−1)/γ} = 259.0 K and ρ_e = 1.359 kg/m³
  • In isentropic flow, falling pressure means falling temperature and density too
In plain words
The isentropic relation p/ρ^γ = constant holds only when there is no shock or friction between the two points.
PART 3/5
03

The normal shock wave

  • A normal shock turns a supersonic flow subsonic
  • Across the wave p, ρ, T jump up; T₀ is unchanged but p₀ falls
  • The process is irreversible: entropy increases
PART 3/5 · 06

The relations and Problems 8.7, 8.8

  • M₂² = [(γ−1)M₁²+2]/[2γM₁²−(γ−1)]; p₂/p₁ = 1 + 2γ(M₁²−1)/(γ+1); ρ₂/ρ₁ = (γ+1)M₁²/[(γ−1)M₁²+2]
  • 8.7 (M₁ = 2.6): M₂ = 0.504, p₂/p₁ = 7.72, T₂/T₁ = 2.238, p₀,₂/p₀,₁ = 0.4601
  • 8.8: p₂/p₁ = 10.33 gives M₁ = 3.000 and T₁ = 1390/2.678 = 519 °R
In plain words
Quick check: the results come out as M₁ = 3.000 and T₁ = 519 °R (standard sea-level temperature). Round numbers like these are a good sign that the calculation is right.
PART 3/5 · 06

Entropy and loss of total pressure (Problem 8.9)

  • s₂ − s₁ = −R ln(p₀,₂/p₀,₁)
  • Problem 8.9: Δs = 199.5 J/(kg·K) gives p₀,₂/p₀,₁ = e^{−Δs/R} = 0.4990 and M₁ = 2.500
  • A weak shock (M₁ near 1) is almost reversible; a strong one loses a lot of p₀
In plain words
Losing p₀ means the flow loses some of its ability to do work. That is why supersonic engine inlets use several oblique shocks first and only then one weak normal shock (module 07).
PART 4/5
04

Measuring speed: the supersonic Pitot tube

  • Subsonic: p₀/p = (1 + 0.2M²)^3.5 (Problem 8.11 in module 03)
  • Supersonic: a bow shock forms ahead of the tube; the tube reads p₀,₂ behind a normal shock, use the Rayleigh formula
  • Using incompressible Bernoulli for compressible flow gives large errors
PART 4/5 · 06

Problem 8.16: Pitot and wall static pressure

  • The Pitot tube reads 1.13 atm and wall static pressure 0.1 atm: p₀,₂/p₁ = 11.3 so M₁ = 2.900
  • Rayleigh formula: p₀,₂/p₁ = [(γ+1)M₁²/2]^{γ/(γ−1)}·[(γ+1)/(2γM₁²−(γ−1))]^{1/(γ−1)}
  • Solving for M₁ from the pressure ratio needs a numerical method or a table
In plain words
The result comes out as M₁ = 2.900, a round number, which is a good sign that the calculation is right.
PART 5/5
05

Meaning for high-speed flight

  • The critical Mach number marks the onset of local supersonic flow on the wing (module 03)
  • Shock waves bring wave drag, buffeting and the nose-down pitch known as Mach tuck
  • Sweepback delays these effects
PART 5/5 · 06

Problem 8.10: behind a strong shock

  • M₁ = 3.1, p₁ = 1800 lb/ft², T₁ = 480 °R: M₂ = 0.470, T₂ = 1343 °R, V₂ = M₂·√(γRT₂) = 843 ft/s
  • The flow behind the wave is subsonic but still very hot and fast
  • This is why supersonic vehicles need heat protection at the nose
In plain words
In practice at large M₁ the gas no longer has constant specific heats; the γ = 1.4 model works well only up to about M = 4 or 5.

History

Rankine, Hugoniot and Rayleigh

William Rankine (1870) and Pierre Hugoniot (1887 to 1889) derived the normal-shock relations independently. The supersonic Pitot formula bears the name of Lord Rayleigh (1910). Anderson tells these milestones in the Historical Note sections of chapters 8 and 9, and recalls that the NACA compressible-flow tables (report TR-1135) were once the "bible" of high-speed aerodynamicists.

Case

Case: reading a Pitot tube at Mach 2.9

  • A Pitot tube in a supersonic wind tunnel reads 1.13 atm while the wall pressure is 0.1 atm.
  • Treating this as incompressible flow and using V = √(2Δp/ρ) would be badly wrong.
  • The Rayleigh formula gives M₁ = 2.900. The calculation has to account for the bow shock ahead of the tube, or the Mach number read would be wrong.
  • (Problem 8.16, data from the book.)
Pitfalls

Common pitfalls

  • p₀ falls across a shock but T₀ stays the same.
  • The ratio p₀,₂/p₁ (Rayleigh formula) differs from p₀,₂/p₀,₁; the two are related through M₁. Anderson 9.3 and 9.4 stress this difference for oblique shocks.
  • To apply normal-shock tables to an oblique shock, use the normal Mach number M₁ sin β.
  • The formulas assume γ = 1.4 and a calorically perfect gas.
  • Getting round Mach numbers (problems 8.8, 8.16) is a good sign, but it is still worth checking with a different method.

Worked problems

Three groups of Anderson problems, solved step by step and compared with the 4th-edition Solution Manual: problems 7.1, 7.4, 7.5, 8.3, 8.5, 8.7, 8.8, 8.9, 8.10, 8.11 and 8.16 all agree (8.7 and 8.11 are solved in module 03).

8.3, 8.5. (8.3) At a point in a flow, T = 300 K, p = 1.2 atm, V = 250 m/s. Find p₀ and T₀. (8.5) An isentropic nozzle has a test section with p = 1 atm, T = 230 K, M = 2. Find p₀ and T₀ in the reservoir.

Anderson 6th, Problems 8.3, 8.5

Show the solution

What the problem says. (8.3) At a point in a flow, T = 300 K, p = 1.2 atm, V = 250 m/s. Find p₀ and T₀. (8.5) An isentropic nozzle has a test section with p = 1 atm, T = 230 K, M = 2. Find p₀ and T₀ in the reservoir.

Idea. Both use the isentropic relations in terms of Mach number: T₀/T = 1 + (γ−1)M²/2 and p₀/p = (T₀/T)^(γ/(γ−1)). If M is not given, get it from V and the speed of sound a = √(γRT).

Steps.

  1. (8.3) a = √(1.4 × 287 × 300) = 347.2 m/s, so M = 250/347.2 = 0.720.
  2. T₀/T = 1 + 0.2M² = 1 + 0.2 × 0.5185 = 1.1037, so T₀ = 300 × 1.1037 = 331.1 K.
  3. p₀/p = (T₀/T)^3.5 = 1.4125, so p₀ = 1.2 × 1.4125 = 1.695 atm.
  4. (8.5) With M = 2: T₀/T = 1 + 0.2 × 4 = 1.8, so T₀ = 230 × 1.8 = 414 K.
  5. p₀/p = 1.8^3.5 = 7.824, so p₀ = 7.824 atm.

Check: The Solution Manual gives the same results. They are plausible: at M = 0.72 (subsonic) T₀ is only about 10% above T and p₀ about 41% above p, while at M = 2 p₀ is nearly 8 times p; both ratios grow quickly with M.

8.8, 8.9. (8.8) Ahead of a normal shock p₁ = 1 atm; behind it p₂ = 10.33 atm and T₂ = 1390 °R. Find M₁ and T₁. (8.9) The entropy increase across a normal shock is 199.5 J/(kg·K). Find the upstream Mach number M₁.

Anderson 6th, Problems 8.8, 8.9

Show the solution

What the problem says. (8.8) Ahead of a normal shock p₁ = 1 atm; behind it p₂ = 10.33 atm and T₂ = 1390 °R. Find M₁ and T₁. (8.9) The entropy increase across a normal shock is 199.5 J/(kg·K). Find the upstream Mach number M₁.

Idea. (8.8) The pressure ratio p₂/p₁ across a normal shock depends only on M₁, so solve for M₁ and then use T₂/T₁ to get T₁. (8.9) The entropy rise is tied to the loss of total pressure: Δs = −R ln(p₀,₂/p₀,₁). From Δs find p₀,₂/p₀,₁, then find the M₁ that gives that ratio.

Steps.

  1. (8.8) p₂/p₁ = 1 + 2γ(M₁² − 1)/(γ + 1) = 10.33, so M₁² = (10.33 − 1)(γ + 1)/(2γ) + 1 = 8.997 and M₁ = 3.000.
  2. At M₁ = 3.000, T₂/T₁ = 2.678, so T₁ = 1390/2.678 = 519 °R.
  3. (8.9) p₀,₂/p₀,₁ = e^(−Δs/R) = e^(−199.5/287) = 0.4990.
  4. The ratio p₀,₂/p₀,₁ is also a function of M₁ (from the normal-shock relations). Solving numerically for p₀,₂/p₀,₁ = 0.4990 gives M₁ = 2.500.

Check: The Solution Manual gives the same results. Problem 8.8 comes out as M₁ = 3.0 and T₁ = 519 °R (standard sea-level temperature) and 8.9 as M₁ = 2.5: round numbers like these are a good sign the calculation is right.

8.10, 8.16. (8.10) A normal shock has M₁ = 3.1, p₁ = 1800 lb/ft², T₁ = 480 °R. Find the flow velocity behind it. (8.16) In a supersonic wind tunnel a Pitot tube reads 1.13 atm and the sidewall static pressure is 0.1 atm. Find the test-section Mach number.

Anderson 6th, Problems 8.10, 8.16

Show the solution

What the problem says. (8.10) A normal shock has M₁ = 3.1, p₁ = 1800 lb/ft², T₁ = 480 °R. Find the flow velocity behind it. (8.16) In a supersonic wind tunnel a Pitot tube reads 1.13 atm and the sidewall static pressure is 0.1 atm. Find the test-section Mach number.

Idea. (8.10) Use the normal-shock relations to find M₂ and T₂, then V₂ = M₂·a₂ with a₂ = √(γRT₂). (8.16) A bow shock stands ahead of the Pitot tube, so the tube reads p₀,₂ (behind a normal shock). The Rayleigh formula relates p₀,₂/p₁ to M₁; solve for M₁ numerically.

Steps.

  1. (8.10) At M₁ = 3.1 the normal shock gives M₂ = 0.470 and T₂/T₁ = 2.799, so T₂ = 480 × 2.799 = 1343 °R.
  2. a₂ = √(γRT₂) = √(1.4 × 1716 × 1343) = 1796 ft/s, so V₂ = M₂ a₂ = 843 ft/s.
  3. (8.16) p₀,₂/p₁ = 1.13/0.1 = 11.3.
  4. Solving the Rayleigh formula p₀,₂/p₁ = [(γ+1)M₁²/2]^(γ/(γ−1)) · [(γ+1)/(2γM₁² − (γ−1))]^(1/(γ−1)) = 11.3 numerically gives M₁ = 2.900.

Check: The Solution Manual gives the same results (8.16 comes out as M₁ ≈ 2.9, a round number). In 8.10 the flow behind the wave is still subsonic (M₂ < 1) as for every normal shock, but it is still very fast and very hot.
