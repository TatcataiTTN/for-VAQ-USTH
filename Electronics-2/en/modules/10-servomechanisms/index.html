Part 1 · Foundations

What a servomechanism is

  • A servo: an electric control system that uses a low-energy input to produce motion or force, usually through an electric or hydraulic motor
  • The heart of automatic flight control (AFCS/autopilot), also used in flight instruments, autothrottle and radar antennas
📖 Explanation for beginners
The pilot sets a small command; the servo amplifies it into enough force to move a control surface.
Part 1 · Foundations

Open loop and closed loop

SystemFeedbackExample
Open loopnoneconventional voltmeter
Closed loopyes: output compared with inputthermostat, autopilot
📖 Explanation for beginners
A closed loop drives the error to zero (null); that is why autopilots always use closed loops.
Part 1 · Foundations

Blocks of a servo loop

  • Follow-up (error detector): compares command and actual position
  • Summing network (resistors) → amplifier → servomotor → gear train → load
📖 Explanation for beginners
When the voltage at the summing point is zero the motor stops.
Part 1 · Foundations

LVDT and RVDT position transducers

  • LVDT: one primary, two secondaries and a moving core; the difference of the secondary voltages is the feedback signal
  • Core centred → equal and opposite voltages → zero output (null); the RVDT measures angle
📖 Explanation for beginners
Only an inductive link, so very durable and stable.

Part 2 · Methods

How a synchro works

  • A synchro is a variable transformer: one primary (rotor) turning 360° and three secondaries (stator) spaced 120°
  • 115 V on the rotor with a 2.2:1 step-down → about 52 V maximum in one stator coil
  • Two systems: torque synchros (light loads, indicators) and control synchros (heavy loads)
📖 Explanation for beginners
The field follows the rotor; a bar magnet placed in it always lines up with the field.
Part 2 · Methods

DC selsyn, autosyn and magnasyn

TypeSupplyRotor
DC selsynDCcontact arm on a resistance winding
MagnasynACpermanent magnet
AutosynACelectromagnet
📖 Explanation for beginners
The receiver has a damper against hunting; at equal angles no current flows.
Part 2 · Methods

The CX–CT control synchro

  • CX (control transmitter) → CT (control transformer): the CT rotor gives an error voltage proportional to the angular difference
  • In phase with the supply for one direction of error, 180° out of phase for the other; null at correspondence
📖 Explanation for beginners
The error signal is amplified so the servomotor turns the load back to null.
Part 2 · Methods

Differential synchros and resolvers

  • TDX: takes two position inputs and gives the sum or difference (reverse S1–S3 to turn adding into subtracting)
  • Resolver: two stator windings 90° apart give sin θ and cos θ; fed to an ADC for the flight computers
📖 Explanation for beginners
The resolver is the most robust angular-measurement device.

  • Hunting, damping

Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

Hunting and damping

  • Hunting: inertia carries the motor past the null, the error reverses phase and the process repeats
  • Damping: generator voltage feedback (proportional to velocity) or a viscous damper (drag cup + magnet flywheel)
📖 Explanation for beginners
Overdamped is slow, underdamped oscillates; a compromise is needed.
Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

Gain, deadband and stability

  • More gain cuts time lag but increases overshoot → damping needed
  • Deadband: the interval around null with no output; narrow → high precision but prone to hunting
  • Reversing the feedback lead can make the system unstable
📖 Explanation for beginners
A servo is a nonlinear system so its behaviour can be unpredictable.
Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

Capacitance sensors and synchro defects

  • Capacitance transmitter: two fixed concentric plates; the dielectric constant changes with the fuel/air ratio
  • Synchro defects: air-gap errors (elliptical stator), unbalanced stator winding
📖 Explanation for beginners
Air-gap errors distort the flux and reduce accuracy.

Three take-aways

  • A servo is negative feedback: command compared with result until the error is zero
  • Synchros transmit angle; the CT turns angular error into an error voltage
  • Stability needs sensible damping

3. History and theory context

Synchros were invented to transmit angles remotely by electricity before digital encoders; feedback servos are the basis of automatic control. Stability theory explains why added gain causes oscillation and why damping is required.

4. Aircraft case study (illustrative)

Illustration following EASA: autopilot command → controller → servo → control surface with a position transducer feeding back; a synchro flap-position indicator. Not a specific system.

5. Exam pitfalls

  • Confusing magnasyn (permanent magnet) with autosyn (electromagnet).
  • Confusing 120° (synchro) with 90° (resolver).
  • Mixing TDX add/subtract: reversing S1–S3 flips the sign.
  • Forgetting that a velocity servo keeps a nonzero error at balance.
