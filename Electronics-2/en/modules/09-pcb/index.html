Part 1 · Foundations

What a PCB is and its types

  • A PCB: thin conductive tracks on a dielectric substrate that also supports the components
  • Three types by layer count: single, double, multi-layered
  • Three connection technologies: through hole, plated through hole, surface mount (SMT)
📖 Explanation for beginners
A PCB saves space and weight compared with loose wiring — very important on aircraft.
Part 1 · Foundations

How the traces are made

  • Base ≈ 1/16 inch (1.5 mm): epoxy-impregnated paper or fibreglass cloth (pre-preg)
  • Heat-press the copper foil → print the pattern with etch-resistive ink → etch away unwanted copper (ammonium persulfate or ferric chloride)
  • The remaining copper is the traces
📖 Explanation for beginners
Like an etching: mask what you want to keep and dissolve the rest.
Part 1 · Foundations

Through holes and plated through holes

  • Plated hole: copper inside the board → lower resistivity at the joint and stiffer
  • Non-plated hole: cheaper and quicker to make
📖 Explanation for beginners
The copper plating in the hole both conducts and holds the lead more firmly.
Part 1 · Foundations

Surface mount and BGA

  • SMT: flat leads soldered to pads on the same side; both sides of the board usable
  • BGA: solder balls under the package, high pin density, special equipment needed
📖 Explanation for beginners
The drawback of SMT is that alignment and soldering are harder.

📖 Explanation for beginners
A via is like a hollow rivet joining layers; typically one layer is Vcc and one is ground.
Part 2 · Methods

Where static electricity comes from

  • Static electricity comes from friction; walking on carpet gives 1 500 V (65 % RH) to 35 000 V (20 % RH)
  • Plastics give 5 000–10 000 V
  • Electrostatic induction: a charged object redistributes charge in another
📖 Explanation for beginners
The lower the humidity the larger the charge on a person.
Part 2 · Methods

ESD susceptibility of devices

DeviceESD susceptibility
Bipolar transistor380 – 7 000 V
CMOS250 – 3 000 V
EPROMas low as 100 V
  • MIL-STD-1686C: Class I = 100–1 000 V; Class II = 1 001–4 000 V
📖 Explanation for beginners
A few hundred volts can kill a part while a person feels only ≳ 3 000 V.

Common pitfalls

  • Invisible ESD
  • Parasitic coupling
  • Controlling the environment
Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

ESD damage is invisible

A part can be more sensitive than you thinkA person feels a spark only from about 3 000 V but CMOS and EPROMs fail at a few hundred volts.
📖 Explanation for beginners
It can cause latent defects that only show up later.
Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

Practical limits and repair

  • At high frequency, capacitive/inductive coupling between layers causes unintended interference → use computer modelling
  • Do not repair PCB solder joints in the field; replace the removable module (LRM)
📖 Explanation for beginners
LRMs are replaced on the line and repaired in an equipped shop.
Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

Environment control

  • Insulating materials (nylon, mylar, vinyl, rubber, mica, ceramics, fibreglass, wood, styrofoam, plastic) store static → keep them out
  • Ionizers neutralize charged insulators
📖 Explanation for beginners
Static cannot be eliminated, only controlled.

3. History and theory context

Printed boards spread when transistors replaced vacuum tubes; miniaturization led to SMT, BGA and multilayer PCBs. The military ESD standard (MIL-STD-1686C) classes component sensitivity.

4. Aircraft case study (illustrative)

Illustrative: replacing an avionics module (LRM) with an ESD-safe procedure. Not a specific system.

5. Exam pitfalls

  • Confusing blind vias (one side) and buried vias (neither side).
  • Confusing flow (through hole) and reflow (SMT) soldering.
  • Forgetting the 1 MΩ resistor in mats and strap.
  • Mixing Class I (100–1 000 V) and Class II (1 001–4 000 V).
