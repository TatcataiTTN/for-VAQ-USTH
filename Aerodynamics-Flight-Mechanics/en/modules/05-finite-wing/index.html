MODULE 05 · Finite wing

Finite wings: tip vortices, downwash and induced drag

Module 02 only said that induced drag comes with lift. This module explains why: the pressure difference between the two wing surfaces makes the air curl round the tip into a vortex; the vortices pull the flow downwards (downwash) and tilt the lift vector backwards. Starting from the vortex model, the Biot-Savart law and Prandtl's lifting-line theory with an elliptical lift distribution, we obtain C_D,i = C_L²/(π·AR). The formula is then extended to any wing with the factors δ and τ, and we re-solve Anderson's Examples 5.1 and 5.2.

Anderson ch. 5 (sec. 5.1–5.3, Examples 5.1, 5.2, Problems 5.1, 5.2) · EASA Module 08, Sub-module 02 (pp. 2.7, 2.14) · Etkin & Reid Appendices B, C

PART 1/5
01

Tip vortices and downwash

  • High pressure under the wing leaks round the tip to the upper surface and forms a vortex
  • The vortices induce a downward velocity (downwash) at the wing
  • The effective angle of attack falls and the lift tilts backwards: this is induced drag
PART 1/5 · 05

Why a finite wing differs from an infinite one

  • An infinite (2D) wing has only c_l and c_d; a finite wing also has spanwise flow and two tip vortices
  • At the trailing edge the upper and lower flows have different directions: a vortex sheet trails behind and rolls up at the tips
  • The downward velocity w at the wing reduces the effective angle of attack, α_eff = α − α_i with α_i = w/V
In plain words
Like a rowing boat: the oar pushes water back and leaves eddies; the wing pushes air down and leaves vortices. The energy left in the vortices is the work paid for by induced drag.
PART 2/5
02

Vortex filament and the Biot-Savart law

  • A vortex filament of strength Γ cannot end in the fluid (Helmholtz theorem)
  • Biot-Savart: dV = Γ/(4π)·dl×r/|r|³
  • A circular vortex loop of radius R: at the centre V = Γ/(2R)
PART 2/5 · 05

A circular vortex loop (Anderson 5.1, 5.2)

  • At the centre of the loop: V = Γ/(2R)
  • At a point on the axis a distance A from the plane of the loop: V = Γ R²/[2(R² + A²)^{3/2}]
  • Checked by numerical integration of Biot-Savart (R = 2, Γ = 1): centre 0.25000 against the formula 0.25000; A = 1.5 gives 0.12800 against 0.12800
In plain words
This is the building block of the lifting-line model: a wing is replaced by a row of horseshoe vortices.
PART 3/5
03

Lifting-line theory and the elliptical distribution

  • Prandtl replaced the wing by a lifting line with a trailing vortex sheet
  • An elliptical lift distribution gives a uniform downwash along the span
  • α_i = C_L/(π·AR) and C_D,i = C_L²/(π·AR): the smallest possible values for a given C_L and AR
PART 3/5 · 05

Results for the elliptical wing

  • Γ(y) = Γ₀ √[1 − (2y/b)²]; downwash w = −Γ₀/(2b) is constant along the span
  • Example C_L = 0.5 and AR = 8: α_i = 1.140° and C_D,i = 0.00995
  • Raising AR to 12 at the same C_L gives C_D,i = 0.00663, a 33% reduction
  • The Spitfire is famous for its near-elliptical wing
In plain words
C_D,i scales with C_L² so slow flight (large C_L) has large induced drag, matching EASA Figure 2-21 (types of drag against speed).
PART 4/5
04

Any wing: the factors δ, τ and e

  • C_D,i = C_L²(1 + δ)/(π·AR) = C_L²/(π·e·AR) with e = 1/(1 + δ)
  • The finite-wing lift slope a = a₀/[1 + (a₀/π·AR)(1 + τ)]
  • A finite wing has a smaller lift slope than its 2D airfoil

PART 4/5 · 05

Example 5.2: changing the aspect ratio from 6 to 10

  • AR = 6, C_D,i = 0.01, δ = 0.055 gives C_L = 0.423, a = 4.485/rad, hence a₀ = 5.988/rad (book: 5.989)
  • AR = 10, δ = τ = 0.105: a = 4.946/rad, C_L = 0.466 (book 0.464), C_D,i = 0.0076 (book 0.0076)
  • Same angle of attack but different C_L, so C_D,i cannot be scaled by AR alone
In plain words
The book itself notes the problem would be simpler if the same C_L were given, since then C_D,i is inversely proportional to AR.
PART 5/5
05

Design consequences: high AR and winglets

  • Gliders have very high AR to reduce induced drag
  • Winglets weaken the tip vortices (EASA Figure 2-20)
  • The wake vortices of large aircraft are the reason for take-off and landing separations
PART 5/5 · 05

Induced drag versus parasite drag

  • Induced drag falls with speed (lower C_L) while parasite drag grows as V²; the total is a minimum at the speed where the two are equal (when C_D,0 = C_D,i)
  • Illustrative example: C_D,0 = 0.02, AR = 8, e = 0.85: the C_L for maximum L/D is √(π·e·AR·C_D,0) = 0.654, (L/D)max = 16.3
  • Winglets improve e without increasing the span
In plain words
The formula (L/D)max = ½√(π·e·AR/C_D,0) shows that a long wing and a clean aircraft (small C_D,0) are the two main levers of performance.

History

Lanchester, Prandtl and the elliptical wing

Frederick Lanchester proposed the idea of wing-tip vortices and induced drag between about 1894 and 1907, but his work was little noticed. Ludwig Prandtl developed lifting-line theory at Göttingen between 1911 and 1918, giving the quantitative formulas Anderson presents in section 5.3; the book's Historical Notes 5.7 and 5.8 tell the story.

Case

Case: lengthening the wing

  • A hypothetical wing flying at C_L = 0.5 with AR = 8 and e = 1 (elliptical) has C_D,i = 0.00995.
  • Redesigned with the same area but AR = 12 at the same C_L, C_D,i = 0.00663, a 33% reduction: 8/12 of the old value.
  • The price: a longer wing is heavier and has a larger root bending moment.
  • The numbers are only illustrative; the formula is from Anderson 5.3.
Pitfalls

Common pitfalls

  • The same angle of attack does not mean the same C_L when AR differs (Example 5.2).
  • The slope a in the formula is per radian; divide by 57.3 to get per degree.
  • δ and τ come from a chart (Anderson Figure 5.20) and depend on AR and taper. Examples 5.1 and 5.2 use two different values.
  • The elliptical distribution is optimal for C_D,i, but real wings have e from about 0.7 to 0.95.
  • Anderson 5.1 and 5.2 need Biot-Savart. The velocity at the centre of the loop is Γ/(2R), not Γ/(4πR).

5.1 – 5.2. A circular vortex loop of radius R and strength Γ: find the induced velocity at the centre and at a point on the axis a distance A from the plane of the loop.

Anderson 6th, Problems 5.1, 5.2

Show the solution

What the problem says. A circular vortex loop of radius R and strength Γ. Find the induced velocity (5.1) at the centre of the loop and (5.2) at a point on the axis a distance A from the plane of the loop.

Idea. Use the Biot-Savart law: each small element dl of the filament induces dV = (Γ/4π)·dl×r/|r|³. Add up (integrate) the contributions around the whole loop.

Steps.

  1. At the centre every element dl is perpendicular to r and r = R, so |dl×r| = R·dθ·R and dV = (Γ/4π)·dθ/R. All contributions point the same way (normal to the plane of the loop); adding them gives V = (Γ/4π)(2π/R) = Γ/(2R).
  2. On the axis, a distance A from the plane, every element dl is at r = √(R² + A²) from the point. By symmetry the components perpendicular to the axis cancel, leaving the axial component, which is dV times R/r.
  3. V = (Γ/4π) · (2πR/r²) · (R/r) = ΓR²/[2(R² + A²)^(3/2)].
  4. With R = 2, Γ = 1: at the centre V = 1/(2 × 2) = 0.25; for A = 1.5: V = 4/[2 × (4 + 2.25)^(3/2)] = 0.12800.

Check: Direct numerical integration of Biot-Savart gives 0.25000 at the centre and 0.12800 at A = 1.5, matching the formulas. The on-axis formula is also consistent: at A = 0 it reduces to Γ/(2R), and for very large A the velocity falls as 1/A³.

Ex. 5.1. AR = 8, δ = τ = 0.055, thin symmetric airfoil, α = 5°: find C_L and C_D,i.

Anderson 6th, Example 5.1

Show the solution

What the problem says. A wing with AR = 8, δ = τ = 0.055, a thin symmetric airfoil (a₀ = 2π per rad) and angle of attack α = 5°. Find C_L and C_D,i.

Idea. A finite wing has a smaller lift slope than its airfoil: a = a₀/[1 + (a₀/πAR)(1 + τ)]. With a, C_L = aα (the airfoil is symmetric so α_L=0 = 0), and then C_D,i = C_L²(1 + δ)/(πAR).

Steps.

  1. a = 2π/[1 + (2π/8π)(1.055)] = 6.2832/1.2638 = 4.972 rad⁻¹, i.e. 0.0868 per degree.
  2. C_L = aα = 0.0868 × 5 = 0.4339.
  3. C_D,i = C_L²(1 + δ)/(πAR) = 0.4339² × 1.055/(8π) = 0.00790.

Check: The book gives a = 4.97 rad⁻¹, C_L = 0.4335 and C_D,i = 0.00789. The last digit differs because the book rounds a = 0.0867 before multiplying by α. C_D,i is small compared with the drag of a whole aircraft, as it should be.

Ex. 5.2. A wing of AR = 6 has C_D,i = 0.01 at α = 3.4° (α_L=0 = −2°, δ = τ = 0.055). Find C_D,i of a wing of AR = 10 (δ = τ = 0.105) with the same airfoil at the same α.

Anderson 6th, Example 5.2

Show the solution

What the problem says. A wing of AR = 6 has C_D,i = 0.01 at α = 3.4° (α_L=0 = −2°, δ = τ = 0.055). Find C_D,i of a wing of AR = 10 (δ = τ = 0.105) with the same airfoil at the same angle of attack.

Idea. The two wings share an airfoil, so they share a₀. From the AR = 6 wing find C_L, then a, then work back to a₀. Use that a₀ to find a and C_L for the AR = 10 wing, and finally C_D,i.

Steps.

  1. AR = 6 wing: C_L = √(πAR·C_D,i/(1 + δ)) = √(π × 6 × 0.01/1.055) = 0.423.
  2. Absolute angle of attack α − α_L=0 = 3.4° + 2° = 5.4° = 0.0942 rad, so a = C_L/(α − α_L=0) = 4.485 rad⁻¹.
  3. From a = a₀/[1 + (1 + τ)a₀/(πAR)] we get a₀ = a/[1 − a(1 + τ)/(πAR)] = 5.988 rad⁻¹.
  4. AR = 10 wing: a = a₀/[1 + (1 + τ)a₀/(πAR)] = 4.946 rad⁻¹ and C_L = a(α − α_L=0) = 4.946 × 0.0942 = 0.466.
  5. C_D,i = C_L²(1 + δ)/(πAR) = 0.466² × 1.105/(10π) = 0.0076.

Check: The book gives a₀ = 5.989, C_L = 0.464 and C_D,i = 0.0076; the small differences in the last digit come from the book rounding at intermediate steps. The two wings have different C_L (0.423 and 0.466), so C_D,i cannot be found from the AR ratio alone.
