03. Refrigeration cycles and aircraft air conditioning

From the reversed Carnot cycle to the vapor-compression cycle, the gas refrigeration cycle and the cooling pack of aircraft air conditioning: COP, R-134a tables, the real cycle.

Part 1 · Foundations

Refrigeration moves heat against its natural direction so it costs work, and a refrigerator and a heat pump are the same machine

Slide Ch.3 p.3 Slide Ch.3 p.9

  • Refrigeration is the transfer of heat from a lower-temperature region to a higher-temperature one; the device is a refrigerator, its cycle a refrigeration cycle, the working fluid a refrigerant (p.3, p.9).
  • The most used cycle is the vapor-compression cycle: the refrigerant is vaporized and condensed alternately and compressed in the vapor phase (p.3).
  • A heat pump also moves heat from cold to warm; refrigerators and heat pumps are essentially the same device and differ only in their objective (p.9).
Refrigerator (R)Heat pump (HP)
Objective (desired output)$Q_L$ removed from the cold space$Q_H$ delivered to the warm space
Heat sourcethe space to be cooledthe cold environment (well water, outdoor air)
Heat sinkthe warm environmentthe house/room to be heated
Coefficient of performance Added from the book: Cengel §11-1
$$\mathrm{COP}_R=\frac{Q_L}{W_{net,in}},\qquad \mathrm{COP}_{HP}=\frac{Q_H}{W_{net,in}}=\mathrm{COP}_R+1$$
  • $W_{net,in}$net work input (from Q_H = Q_L + W)
  • $\mathrm{COP}>1$can exceed 1: COP is not a thermal efficiency
📖 Explanation for beginners
Heat flows from hot to cold by itself. To go the other way a machine must consume work, like pumping water uphill. A refrigerator counts the heat removed from the cold compartment as its product; a heat pump counts the heat delivered to the house. Because the work also ends up as rejected heat, Q_H = Q_L + W and a heat pump's COP exceeds the refrigerator's by exactly 1. 1 ton of refrigeration = 211 kJ/min (Cengel p.609): a 3-ton machine has about 10.5 kW of cooling capacity.
Part 1 · Foundations

A refrigerant absorbs a great deal of heat during a phase change at constant temperature: the vapor-compression cycle exploits exactly that

Slide Ch.3 p.4–7

  • When a body is heated either its temperature rises or its aggregate state changes: a phase transition (p.4). During the phase change the temperature stays constant; only after it is complete does more heat raise the temperature (p.5).
  • A phase change needs heat supplied to or withdrawn from the environment; the direction depends on the transition (table p.6).
ProcessState changeEnergy flowIn the vapor-compression cycle

Meltingsolid → liquidabsorbs heat—
Evaporationliquid → gasabsorbs heatevaporator: receives Q_L

Sublimationsolid → gasabsorbs heat—
Desublimationgas → solidemits heat—
Numbers for water at 1 atm (Table A-4) Added from the book: Table A-4

Warming 1 kg of liquid water from 0 to 100 °C takes 419.2 kJ; evaporating 1 kg at 100 °C takes h_fg = 2,256.4 kJ, 5.4 times more. The plot on p.7 is a sketch not to scale (the horizontal segment looks shorter than the heating segment). This is why the refrigeration cycle exploits evaporation/condensation: one kilogram of refrigerant carries a lot of heat at nearly constant temperature.

The title of p.4 is "Specific heat capacity and latent heat" but the slide defines neither specific heat nor latent heat (see the source-error list).

📖 Explanation for beginners
Heat water and its temperature rises until 100 °C and then stops while it boils: the heat supplied no longer warms it but breaks the bonds between molecules (latent heat). A refrigerator does the reverse: it lets the refrigerant boil at a very low temperature in the evaporator to absorb heat from the cold compartment, then compresses it to high pressure and condenses it in the condenser to dump the heat outside.
Part 1 · Foundations

The phase change happens gradually in the wet-vapour region between the saturated-liquid line x = 0 and the saturated-vapour line x = 1

Slide Ch.3 p.8

Redrawn with CoolProp (R-134a, ASHRAE reference) for slide p.8; the slide does not name the fluid. (click the image for full size)
  • Real substances exist in all three states; the gas ↔ liquid change is used by the compression system (p.8). The change does not happen abruptly but gradually in the wet-vapour region.
  • The wet region lies between the boiling line (x = 0, saturated liquid) and the saturated-vapour line (x = 1); the lines x = 0.2…0.8 are the vapor mass fraction.
  • In the wet region an isotherm (T = 273 K, 293 K on the slide) is horizontal: temperature and saturation pressure are tied together. Qualitative description only, no numbers are read from the diagram.

Illustration if the fluid is R-134a (the slide does not say): P_sat at 0 °C = 293.0 kPa and at 20 °C = 572.1 kPa (Table A-11), so the 293 K line lies at about 1.95 times the pressure of the 273 K line.

📖 Explanation for beginners
Think of the diagram as a three-region map: liquid on the left, vapour on the right, a liquid–vapour mixture in the middle. The quality x tells what mass fraction of the mixture is vapour. On the log p–h map (pressure on a log scale, enthalpy linear) the horizontal distance between two points is an enthalpy difference, i.e. the heat or work to be computed.
Part 1 · Foundations

The reversed Carnot cycle is the COP ceiling between two temperatures but cannot be built, so we use the vapor-compression cycle

Slide Ch.3 p.10–14

Redrawn from CoolProp with the data of Cengel Problem 11–2 (T_H = 30 °C, evaporator pressure 160 kPa); state numbers as on slide p.12. (click the image for full size)
  • Carnot is a totally reversible cycle of two isothermal and two isentropic processes (p.10). The slide draws it as an engine (clockwise, receiving Q_in at T_o and giving W_out). Reversed, every heat and work interaction changes direction: a Carnot refrigerator/heat pump (p.11).
  • 1→2 the refrigerant absorbs Q_L isothermally at T_L; 2→3 isentropic compression (temperature rises to T_H); 3→4 rejects Q_H isothermally at T_H (saturated vapor → saturated liquid); 4→1 isentropic expansion back to T_L (p.12).
COP of a reversed Carnot machine (p.13), temperatures in K
$$\mathrm{COP}_{R,Carnot}=\frac{1}{T_H/T_L-1},\qquad \mathrm{COP}_{HP,Carnot}=\frac{1}{1-T_L/T_H}$$
  • It is the most efficient refrigeration cycle between two temperature levels; ideal but impractical, used only as a standard of comparison for real cycles (p.14). The COP rises when T_L rises or T_H falls.
  • Why it cannot be built Added from the book: Cengel §11-2: 2→3 would compress a liquid–vapor mixture and 4→1 would expand a high-moisture fluid in a turbine; outside the dome it is hard to keep the processes isothermal.
📖 Explanation for beginners
Numerical example: T_L = −10 °C and T_H = 35 °C: COP_R = 263.15/45 = 5.85; COP_HP = 6.85. The farther apart the two temperatures, the lower the COP. Always convert °C to kelvin first (the formula uses the ratio of absolute temperatures).

Part 2 · Methods

Replacing the turbine with a valve and vaporizing completely before compression turns the reversed Carnot cycle into a usable one

Slide Ch.3 p.15–17

Redrawn from CoolProp with the Tutorials I states; 3→4 on T–s is dashed because irreversible throttling has no defined path; 4s is the point reached by isentropic expansion (slide p.15 labels it 4′). (click the image for full size)
  • Two changes from the reversed Carnot cycle (p.15): vaporize completely before compression and replace the turbine with a throttling valve or capillary tube. The result is the ideal vapor-compression cycle, the most widely used for refrigerators, air conditioning and heat pumps (p.16).
ProcessDeviceDescription (p.16–17)State
1→2compressorisentropic compression to condenser pressure; temperature far above the surroundings1: saturated vapor; 2: superheated vapor
2→3condenserisobaric heat rejection Q_H to saturated liquid (still hotter than the surroundings)3: saturated liquid
3→4throttling valvepressure falls to the evaporator pressure; temperature drops below the cold space4: low-quality saturated mixture
4→1evaporatorcomplete isobaric evaporation by absorbing Q_L1: saturated vapor
📖 Explanation for beginners
A compressor cannot take a liquid–vapour mixture, so the refrigerant is forced to vaporize completely before compression. A turbine expanding a wet mixture is hard to build, so a narrow valve replaces it: some recovered work is lost but it is far simpler. The price is that 3→4 is irreversible (entropy rises), so the cycle is no longer reversible.
Part 2 · Methods

The COP of the vapor-compression cycle needs only four enthalpies: (h₁ − h₄)/(h₂ − h₁)

Slide Ch.3 p.19–20

  • All four are steady-flow devices; kinetic and potential energy changes of the refrigerant are small compared with work and heat and are neglected; the condenser and evaporator involve no work; the compressor is adiabatic (p.19).
Steady-flow energy equation per kg (p.19)
$$(q_{in}-q_{out})+(w_{in}-w_{out})=h_e-h_i$$
DeviceReduced equationQuantity
Compressor 1→2$w_{in}=h_2-h_1$specific compressor work (p.22)
Condenser 2→3$q_H=h_2-h_3$specific rejected heat (p.23)
Throttle 3→4$h_4=h_3$isenthalpic (p.24)
Evaporator 4→1$q_L=h_1-h_4$specific refrigerating capacity (p.25)
COP from four enthalpies (p.20)
$$\mathrm{COP}_R=\frac{q_L}{w_{net,in}}=\frac{h_1-h_4}{h_2-h_1},\qquad \mathrm{COP}_{HP}=\frac{q_H}{w_{net,in}}=\frac{h_2-h_3}{h_2-h_1}$$

States of the ideal cycle (Cengel §11-3): h₁ = h_g at P₁ (saturated vapor), h₃ = h_f at P₃ (saturated liquid), s₂ = s₁ (isentropic compression). Slides p.22–25 write the powers as P_V, Q̇_c, Q̇₀ and the flow as ṁ_R (see the source-error list on the symbols).

📖 Explanation for beginners
Each device is a "box" with energy in and out as enthalpy. A box with no work has heat = enthalpy difference; the throttle has neither work nor heat so its enthalpy stays unchanged. So once h₁, h₂, h₃ (h₄ = h₃) are looked up, everything follows: cooling heat, rejected heat, work, COP.
Part 2 · Methods

On the log p–h diagram, heat and work are lengths of horizontal segments, so the whole cycle can be read with a ruler

Slide Ch.3 p.18 Slide Ch.3 p.21–25

  • The log p–h diagram is used to calculate refrigeration systems: measured temperatures and pressures are plotted and enthalpy differences read off; results are found graphically (p.21). Advantage: energy differences appear directly as line segments; every refrigerant has its own diagram (p.18).
  • Three of the four processes are straight lines; heat in the condenser and evaporator is proportional to the length of the horizontal segment; only compression 1→2 is a curve (p.18, figures of p.22–25).

📖 Explanation for beginners
Refrigeration engineers use this diagram because it turns an enthalpy subtraction into "measure a segment": segment 4–1 is the cooling effect, segment 1–2 the compression work, segment 2–3 the rejected heat. The COP is the ratio of two segments. In this course the diagram only gives the shape of the cycle; the numbers come from tables so that their error is under control.
Part 2 · Methods

A four-step recipe: h₁ and h₃ read straight from the saturation table, h₂ interpolated in s, h₄ = h₃

Tutorials I p.24–27 Added from the book: Cengel §11-3

  • Step 1 h₁ = h_g and s₁ = s_g at P₁ (Table A-12, by pressure). Step 2 h₃ = h_f at P₃ (A-12), h₄ = h₃. Step 3 h₂ at P₂ and s = s₁: take the P₂ block of Table A-13 and interpolate linearly in s between the two rows around s₁ (the saturated row is the first row of the block). Step 4 q_L = h₁ − h₄; w = h₂ − h₁; q_H = h₂ − h₃; COP.
  • Printed pages (Appendix 1): A-11 and A-12 pp.928–930, A-13 pp.931–932. The site library quotes the table and page it used.

📖 Explanation for beginners
The only hard part is h₂: the compressor is isentropic so entropy out equals entropy in. We know s₁ (from state 1) and the outlet pressure P₂, so we find in the superheated table at P₂ the place where the entropy equals s₁ and read the enthalpy there. Linear interpolation is the book's method; its difference from the equation of state is tiny, which is why the printed answers agree to the third digit.
Part 2 · Methods

The COP of the ideal vapor-compression cycle is always below Carnot and rises 2–4 % per °C of higher evaporating temperature

Computed with CoolProp; T_H = 40 °C (condensing); the ideal cycle is below Carnot for every T_L. (click the image for full size)
  • The Tutorials I cycle (0.14–0.8 MPa: T_sat = −18.77 and 31.31 °C) has COP = 3.97, while Carnot between these two temperatures is 5.08: it reaches 78 % (irreversible throttling and superheated-vapor compression).
  • Rule of thumb Added from the book: Cengel §11-3: the COP rises by about 2–4 % per °C of higher evaporating temperature or lower condensing temperature; checked with CoolProp on a 12-point grid: 2.3 to 3.8 % per °C.
  • Design consequence: do not run the evaporator colder than necessary and keep the condenser clean/ventilated so the condensing pressure stays low.
📖 Explanation for beginners
Carnot gives the theoretical limit; the vapor-compression cycle is the practical one. The gap between the two curves is the "price" of the throttling valve and of compressing superheated vapour. The practical point: the COP is very sensitive to the operating temperatures, so a freezer set at −30 °C costs far more electricity than one set at −5 °C.

Two natural questions once you have the COP: (1) how does it compare with the theoretical limit? About 78 %, quite good. (2) can it be improved? A turbine instead of the valve recovers expansion work and sends colder refrigerant (lower enthalpy, so it absorbs more heat) to the evaporator, but a turbine for a wet mixture is expensive and complex.

Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

The real cycle differs from the ideal one by pressure drops, superheat, subcooling and non-isentropic compression, and these effects can raise or lower the COP

Slide Ch.3 p.26 Added from the book: Cengel §11-4

  • Slide p.26: the ideal cycle assumes no losses; in practice this is not possible. The real-cycle log p–h figure (red) has slightly slanted horizontals, a condenser exit to the left of the saturated-liquid line and a compressor inlet to the right of the saturated-vapor line (read qualitatively from the figure).
  • Cengel §11-4 explains: friction (pressure drops in the exchangers and lines) and heat transfer to or from the surroundings are the two sources of irreversibility. Slight superheat at the compressor inlet makes sure the fluid is fully vaporized; the long line gains heat and loses pressure, raising the specific volume and so the compressor work; real compression with friction raises the entropy (1→2) or, if cooled during compression, lowers it (1→2′); a little subcooling before the valve is welcome because the fluid enters the evaporator with a lower enthalpy.

subcooling after the condenserincreases (lower h₄)unchanged

📖 Explanation for beginners

The gas refrigeration cycle gives COP = 1/(r_p^((k−1)/k) − 1) and explains why air expanding through a turbine cools to −9 °C in Problem 11–97

Added from the book: Cengel §11-8 (the book does not describe the cooling pack; this is the nearest theory)

Drawn: ideal-gas air, c_p = 1.005, k = 1.4; 1→2 compression, 2→3 cooling, 3→4 expansion (turbine), 4→1 heat absorption Q_L. (click the image for full size)
  • The gas refrigeration cycle is the reversed Brayton cycle: compression 1→2, isobaric cooling 2→3, turbine expansion 3→4, heat absorption 4→1. Cengel Fig. 11–18 shows an open-cycle aircraft cooling system: atmosphere → compressor → cooled by the surrounding air → turbine → straight into the cabin; advantage: simple, light components.
  • With constant c_p, k: $\mathrm{COP}_R=\dfrac{q_L}{w_{comp}-w_{turb}}=\dfrac{1}{r_p^{(k-1)/k}-1}$ (depends only on r_p; checked with sympy); T₄ = T₃/r_p^((k−1)/k).
  • Problem 11–97 (aircraft on the ground): 30 °C, 100 kPa → 250 kPa, cooled to 70 °C: T₂ = 120.7 °C; T₄ = −9.0 °C ≈ −9 °C (printed −9 °C). COP = 1/(2.5^0.2857 − 1) = 3.34. Regeneration (Problem 11–61: lowest temperature −99.4 °C; COP = 1.12) lowers T₃ below the ambient temperature.
Where the ideal model differs from the real pack

The pack on the slides adds: two stages of ram-air cooling (primary and secondary) around the compressor, water extractors, a reheater and a condenser using the cold air leaving the turbine, and a hot-air mixing valve; the ideal model has only a compressor, one heat exchanger and a turbine. Because the slides give no pressure ratio or ACM efficiency, the model is used only to understand the trend (heat up, reject heat, cold expansion), not to compute the pack.

📖 Explanation for beginners
See the cooling pack as a reversed Brayton cycle with accessories: compression (with cooling before and after), expansion to obtain the cold. The COP of the gas cycle is lower than the vapor cycle's (at r_p = 2.5 only about 3.3 and falling fast as r_p grows) but the equipment is light, simple and uses the engine's own bleed air, which suits aircraft very well.

3. Theory and history context

Cengel §11-3 (p.612) recounts: in 1834 Jacob Perkins (English) received a patent for a closed-cycle ice machine using ether or other volatile fluids; in 1850 Alexander Twining began to build vapor-compression ice machines using ethyl ether; by the 1890s small electric-motor-driven machines with automatic controls replaced the older steam-driven units; by 1930 vapor-compression systems were relatively efficient, reliable, small and inexpensive. The Ch.3 slides have no history. The vapor-compression cycle is essentially a Rankine cycle run in reverse (Cengel §11-8, p.628) and the gas refrigeration cycle is a reversed Brayton cycle. Cengel Problem 11–125 mentions R-134a replacing R-12 as ozone-friendly (only in the statement, not used).

Computed with CoolProp (T_H = 40 °C).

A unified view: a refrigerator is always a heat engine run backwards, so each cycle of Chapter 2 (Carnot, Rankine, Brayton) has a cold "sibling": reversed Carnot, vapor-compression (reversed Rankine with a valve instead of a turbine) and gas refrigeration (reversed Brayton). Two important differences: a refrigerant is chosen to change phase at suitable temperatures/pressures (R-134a evaporates at −18.77 °C at 0.14 MPa), while the gas cycle keeps a gas phase so its COP is lower but the equipment is light.

4. Aircraft application

From the slides (pp.29–36, every page viewed as an image): air cycle is the most used air conditioning on turbine passenger jets; the pack takes bleed air (10th stage of both engines, APU or external source per the p.29 figure), through the ozone converter, flow control valve, primary heat exchanger (≈ 150 °C, 30–40 psi, drop ≈ 100 °C), ACM compressor (+30–40 °C), secondary heat exchanger (−60…70 °C), water extractor, reheater, turbine (→ −10 °C, 40,000–50,000 rpm), condenser, and a mixing box with a hot-air mixing valve. 5. Exam pitfalls

  • h₄ = h₃ (saturated liquid at the HIGH pressure); never h₄ = h_f or h_g at the low pressure. x₄ is evaluated at the low pressure.
  • h₂ is interpolated in s (isentropic compressor), not in T; real compressor: h₂ = h₁ + (h₂ₛ − h₁)/η_c.
  • Q̇ = ṁ·Δh: forgetting ṁ leaves kJ/kg; cooling-load conversion: 1 ton = 211 kJ/min = 3.517 kW; 300 kJ/min = 5 kW.
  • Carnot uses K; COP_R = T_L/(T_H − T_L); COP_HP = COP_R + 1. The COP of the vapor-compression cycle is always below Carnot between the two saturation temperatures.
  • Gas cycle: T₄ = T₃/r_p^((k−1)/k) (T₃ is the turbine INLET temperature); the simple COP depends only on r_p; net work = compressor work − turbine work (do not forget to subtract).
  • Cooling pack: answer only from the slides (approximate temperatures, order of the components); do not invent numbers the slides lack (pressure ratio, ACM efficiency). For "20 to 10 °C" say it is written in reverse and read 10–20 °C.
  • Printed answers may be wrong or statements inconsistent (Cengel 11–14 COP +25.7 % not 4.2 %; 11–22 ideal cycle and T₂ = 60 °C at once): check with an energy balance and against Carnot.
