MODULE 07 · Oblique shocks

Oblique shocks, Prandtl-Meyer expansion waves and the supersonic airfoil

When a supersonic flow is turned through an angle it forms an oblique shock (compression) or a Prandtl-Meyer expansion fan. This module solves the ch. 9 problems of Anderson: Mach angle, the θ-β-M relation, maximum deflection and detached shocks; total-pressure loss in multi-wave systems (supersonic inlets); the maximum Prandtl-Meyer turn of 130.45° from sonic flow; and shock-expansion theory for a flat plate at M = 2.6. Every number is computed directly from the formulas, with no tables.

Anderson ch. 9 (Problems 9.1, 9.2, 9.4, 9.5, 9.6, 9.8, 9.13, 9.15, 9.20) · EASA Module 08, Sub-module 03 (p. 3.13, Figure 3-11)

PART 1/5
01

Mach waves and oblique shocks

  • A small disturbance in supersonic flow makes a Mach wave at angle μ = arcsin(1/M)
  • A finite disturbance makes an oblique shock at β > μ
  • An oblique shock is a normal shock for the normal component M₁ sin β

PART 2/5
02

The θ-β-M relation and maximum deflection

  • tan θ = 2cot β·(M₁²sin²β − 1)/[M₁²(γ + cos 2β) + 2]
  • For a given M₁ there is a maximum deflection θ_max; beyond it the shock detaches and curves ahead of the body
  • Every θ < θ_max has two solutions β: the weak (usual) and the strong shock
PART 2/5 · 07

Problems 9.5 and 9.6

  • 9.5: 22.2° half-angle wedge, M₁ = 2.5: β = 45.89°, p₂ = 3.59 atm, T₂ = 458 K, M₂ = 1.537
  • 9.6: at M = 2.4, θ_max = 28.68° is the largest angle of attack with the shock still attached at the leading edge, and the maximum surface pressure is then p₂/p₁ = 5.32
  • β is the root of a trigonometric equation; here it is found by bisection
In plain words
A 22.2° deflection at M 2.5 is fairly close to θ_max (≈ 29.8°) so the shock is quite strong; more would detach it.
PART 3/5
03

Total-pressure loss and the supersonic inlet

  • Slowing a supersonic flow with several oblique shocks and then a weak normal shock loses less p₀ than one strong normal shock
  • For an oblique shock use p₀,₂/p₀,₁ with M_n1; the p₀,₂/p₁ column of the normal-shock table cannot be used (Problems 9.3, 9.4)
  • This is the principle of the supersonic engine inlet

PART 4/5
04

Prandtl-Meyer expansion waves

  • Supersonic flow turning round a convex corner expands through a fan of Mach waves with no loss in p₀
  • The Prandtl-Meyer angle ν(M): θ = ν(M₂) − ν(M₁)
  • The maximum turn from sonic flow is ν(∞) = 130.45°
PART 4/5 · 07

Problems 9.15 and 9.20

  • 9.15: ν(∞) = (√((γ+1)/(γ−1)) − 1)·90° = 130.45°
  • 9.20: M₁ = 3, p₁ = 1 atm, a 20° expansion then a 20° compression: after the expansion M = 4.318, p/p₁ = 0.160; after the compression M₃ = 2.725, p₃ = 0.918 atm
  • p₃ ≠ p₁ although the flow direction is the same: the compression shock loses p₀ while the expansion does not
In plain words
Problem 9.20 asks why the final pressure differs from the initial one. The reason is that the shock is irreversible and entropy rises, so the original state cannot be recovered.
PART 5/5
05

The supersonic flat plate: shock-expansion theory

  • The lower surface gets an oblique shock (pressure up), the upper surface an expansion (pressure down)
  • Lift c_l = (p_l − p_u)cos α/q and wave drag c_d,w = (p_l − p_u)sin α/q
  • Ackeret's linear theory: c_l = 4α/√(M² − 1)

History

Mach, Prandtl, Meyer and Ackeret

In 1887 Ernst Mach photographed a supersonic bullet with its shock waves; Anderson devotes a Historical Note (9.11) to him. Theodor Meyer, a student of Prandtl, gave the expansion-wave solution in his 1908 dissertation, hence the name Prandtl-Meyer. Jakob Ackeret published the linear theory for supersonic airfoils in 1925. The NACA compressible-flow tables TR-1135 (1953) collect these results.

Case

Case: slowing an M = 4 flow at an inlet

  • A hypothetical inlet receives M = 4 flow. With one normal shock only 13.9% of the total pressure remains.
  • Adding a 25.3° oblique shock ahead raises it to 31.4%, and a further 20° oblique shock to 42.0% (Anderson 9.8).
  • That is why supersonic aircraft inlets have a multi-step cone or ramp.
  • The numbers come from the book problem, not from a specific inlet.
Pitfalls

Common pitfalls

  • The Mach number behind an oblique shock is M₂ = M_n2/sin(β − θ), not M_n2.
  • Choose the correct root β (weak or strong); the weak one is the usual case.
  • The p₀,₂/p₁ column of the table applies only to a normal shock, so it cannot be used with the M_n1 of an oblique shock to find p₀,₂ (Problems 9.3, 9.4).
  • After an expansion and an equal compression the pressure does not return to its original value, because the compression shock loses p₀ (Problem 9.20).
  • Ackeret theory is for small angles only.

9.2. An oblique shock with wave angle β = 30° in an M₁ = 4 flow, with upstream pressure and temperature p₁ = 2.65×10⁴ N/m², T₁ = 223.3 K. Find the pressure, temperature, Mach number, total pressure and total temperature behind the wave, and the entropy increase across it.

Anderson 6th, Problem 9.2

Show the solution

What the problem says. An oblique shock with wave angle β = 30° in an M₁ = 4 flow (p₁ = 2.65×10⁴ N/m², T₁ = 223.3 K). Find p₂, T₂, M₂, p₀,₂, T₀,₂ and the entropy increase.

Idea. An oblique shock acts as a normal shock only for the velocity component normal to the wave. So take M_n1 = M₁ sin β and use the normal-shock relations at M_n1 for the ratios p, T, p₀. The Mach number behind the wave must be found from M_n2 and the deflection angle θ.

Steps.

  1. M_n1 = M₁ sin β = 4 sin 30° = 2.000.
  2. Normal shock at M = 2: p₂/p₁ = 4.5; T₂/T₁ = 1.6875; p₀,₂/p₀,₁ = 0.7209; M_n2 = 0.577.
  3. From the θ-β-M relation, θ = 17.78°. Mach number behind the wave: M₂ = M_n2/sin(β − θ) = 0.577/sin(12.22°) = 2.728.
  4. p₂ = 4.5 × 2.65×10⁴ = 119 250 N/m²; T₂ = 1.6875 × 223.3 = 376.8 K.
  5. p₀,₁ = p₁ (p₀/p at M = 4) = 4 023 633 N/m²; p₀,₂ = p₀,₁ × 0.7209 = 2 900 532 N/m².
  6. T₀ is unchanged across the wave: T₀,₂ = T₀,₁ = 223.3 × 4.2 = 937.9 K. Entropy: Δs = −R ln(p₀,₂/p₀,₁) = −287 ln(0.7209) = 93.9 J/(kg·K).

Check: The Solution Manual gives the same results. Quick check: M_n1 = 2.000 is a round number; M₂ = 2.73 is still above 1 because β = 30° is a weak shock; Δs > 0 as for every shock.

9.5, 9.8. (9.5) A flow with M₁ = 2.5, p₁ = 1 atm, T₁ = 300 K passes over a 22.2° half-angle wedge. Find the wave angle β, p₂, T₂ and M₂. (9.8) Compare the p₀ retained by three shock systems at M = 4: one normal shock; a 25.3° oblique shock then a normal shock; 25.3° and 20° oblique shocks then a normal shock.

Anderson 6th, Problems 9.5, 9.8

Show the solution

What the problem says. (9.5) A flow with M₁ = 2.5, p₁ = 1 atm, T₁ = 300 K passes over a 22.2° half-angle wedge. Find the wave angle β, p₂, T₂ and M₂. (9.8) Compare the p₀ retained by three shock systems at M = 4: one normal shock; a 25.3° oblique shock then a normal shock; 25.3° and 20° oblique shocks then a normal shock.

Idea. (9.5) Given M₁ and the wedge angle θ, solve the θ-β-M relation for β (take the weak-shock root), then find the ratios across the wave with M_n1 = M₁ sin β. (9.8) The p₀ retained after several waves in a row is the product of the p₀,₂/p₀,₁ ratios of each wave.

Steps.

  1. (9.5) Solving θ-β-M with θ = 22.2°, M₁ = 2.5 (weak root): β = 45.89°.
  2. M_n1 = 2.5 sin β = 1.795. The normal shock at M_n1 gives p₂/p₁ = 3.592 and T₂/T₁ = 1.528.
  3. p₂ = 3.59 atm; T₂ = 300 × 1.528 = 458 K; M₂ = M_n2/sin(β − θ) = 1.537.
  4. (9.8) (a) One normal shock at M = 4: p₀,₂/p₀,₁ = 0.139.
  5. (b) A 25.3° oblique shock (p₀ left 0.496, M₂ = 2.188) then a normal shock (0.634): 0.496 × 0.634 = 0.314.
  6. (c) Adding a 20° oblique shock (p₀ left 0.883, M₃ = 1.393): 0.496 × 0.883 × 0.960 = 0.420.

Check: Two points when comparing with the Solution Manual (4th edition). Its problem 9.5 uses a 22.5° wedge and gets β = 46° and p₂ = 3.613 atm; with 22.2° we get 45.89° and 3.592 atm. Problem 9.8 differs by under 1% because the book reads values from charts. Adding oblique shocks raises the retained p₀ from 14% to 42%.

9.13, 9.20. (9.13) An infinitely thin flat plate at angle of attack α in an M = 2.6 flow. Find c_l and c_d,w at α = 5°, 15° and 30°. (9.20) A flow at M = 3, p = 1 atm expands through 20° then is compressed through 20° back to the original direction. Find the final Mach number and pressure, and explain why the pressure does not return to 1 atm.

Anderson 6th, Problems 9.13, 9.20

Show the solution

What the problem says. (9.13) An infinitely thin flat plate at angle of attack α in an M = 2.6 flow. Find c_l and c_d,w at α = 5°, 15° and 30°. (9.20) A flow at M = 3, p = 1 atm expands through 20° then is compressed through 20° back to the original direction. Find the final Mach number and pressure, and explain why the pressure does not return to 1 atm.

Idea. (9.13) The lower surface gets an oblique shock (pressure up) and the upper surface an expansion (pressure down). Divide the pressure difference p_l − p_u by the dynamic pressure q = γM²p/2, then project onto the directions normal and parallel to the flow with cos α and sin α. (9.20) Work in order: Prandtl-Meyer expansion (isentropic), then an oblique compression shock.

Steps.

  1. (9.13) For each α: on the lower surface θ = α and p_l/p comes from the oblique shock; on the upper surface ν(M_u) = ν(2.6) + α and p_u/p comes from the isentropic relation.
  2. c_l = (p_l − p_u) cos α/q and c_d,w = (p_l − p_u) sin α/q.
  3. α = 5°: c_l = 0.146, c_d,w = 0.013 (Ackeret: c_l = 0.145).
  4. α = 15°: c_l = 0.453, c_d,w = 0.121 (Ackeret: c_l = 0.436).
  5. α = 30°: c_l = 1.025, c_d,w = 0.592 (Ackeret: c_l = 0.873).
  6. (9.20) ν(3) = 49.76°. After the 20° expansion: ν = 69.76°, so M = 4.318 and p/p₁ = (p₀/p)₃/(p₀/p)_M = 0.160.
  7. 20° compression by an oblique shock at M = 4.318: β = 31.44°, p₃/p = 5.751, M₃ = 2.725. So p₃ = 0.160 × 5.751 = 0.918 atm.

Check: The Solution Manual agrees at 5° and 15°. At 30° it prints c_l = 1.19 and c_d,w = 0.687 because it drops the cos α factor; the correct values are 1.02 and 0.59. Ackeret matches the exact result at 5° but is about 17% low at 30°. In 9.20, p₃ ≠ 1 atm because the compression shock loses p₀ while the expansion does not.
