13. Aircraft data networks, AFDX and IMA

The data networks of a modern aircraft: LANs and ethernet, AFDX (ARINC 664) with virtual links, the BAG, frames and redundancy, then IMA putting many avionic applications on one shared computing platform. Built from Tooley Chapters 16 and 18 (no lecturer slides; Module 05 only mentions AFDX).

Lecture

PART 1/3
01

A LAN shares or switches its medium; multiple-access ethernet needs CSMA/CD while switched full-duplex has no collisions

  • Four LAN topologies
  • Ethernet, CSMA/CD and binary exponential back-off

A LAN is a high-rate, short-range network with four topologies: star, ring, tree and bus

(Tooley 16.2, 16.3.) A LAN is confined to one site, in avionics one aircraft; it should follow an international standard, run at a high rate (e.g. 10 to 100 Mbps), reach several hundred metres on low-cost light cabling, support many independent nodes, be reliable, rely little on centralised parts and be easy to expand.

TopologyHow it works
Stara central switching element gives a dedicated path between two nodes
Ringa closed loop; a node waits its turn and sends a packet with source and destination addresses
Tree and busmultiple-access medium: only one device transmits at a time; a bus is a tree with one trunk and no branches
📖 Explanation for beginners

Multiple-access topologies (bus, tree) need a collision-handling scheme; a switched star makes every link point to point. AFDX chose the cascaded star.

Multiple-access ethernet uses CSMA/CD and a random back-off that doubles after each collision; switched full-duplex removes collisions

Ethernet follows IEEE 802.3. CSMA/CD: carrier sense (know whether the bus is idle or busy), multiple access (several hosts share the bus), collision detection. Rules: if the bus is idle send, otherwise wait; after a collision wait and resend. When both hosts must resend each picks a random instant within an interval, and if they collide again the interval doubles: binary exponential back-off.

Interval after collision n
$$I_n=2^{\,n-1}\,T\qquad(n=3:\;4T,\;n=4:\;8T)$$

The bus arrangement is half-duplex; a switched arrangement allows full-duplex: sending and receiving at the same time (Tooley 16.4).

📖 Explanation for beginners

What commercial ethernet does not guarantee is the maximum transfer time: after many collisions critical data can arrive too late. That is why aviation needs a deterministic network.

PART 2/3
02

AFDX adds determinism and guaranteed bandwidth to switched ethernet with virtual links, the BAG and sequenced frames

  • Switch, end system and determinism
  • Virtual links and the BAG
  • The AFDX frame and reliability

AFDX (ARINC 664) uses store-and-forward switches so collisions disappear, and adds deterministic QoS

(Tooley 16.5, 16.6.) AFDX implements ARINC 664, based on IEEE 802.3 ethernet and commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) parts; developed for the Airbus A380, now on the Boeing 787. A cascaded star: avionic systems connect by full-duplex links (two twisted pairs) to a switch; the switch CPU reads the destination address, puts the packet in the forwarding table and copies it to the TX buffer. Every packet is buffered so there are no collisions. Determinism means a guaranteed maximum delay from node to node; QoS is the ability to guarantee a level of performance of the data flow. The end system is the interface between the avionics hardware and the network.

📖 Explanation for beginners

With large enough buffers a packet may be delayed by congestion but not lost by overflow. If high-speed ARINC 429 is 100 kbit/s then a 100 Mbit/s network is 1,000 times faster: matching Tooley 18.5's "one thousand times the bandwidth".

Virtual links (VL) share the physical bandwidth and the BAG limits the send rate: maximum bit rate = $L_{max}\times8\times1000/\text{BAG}$

(Tooley 16.7, 16.8.) A VL is a one-way logical channel from exactly one end system to one or more others; packets are routed by a 16-bit VL ID (in the last two bytes of the destination MAC address, after a 32-bit constant field). The VLs' total bandwidth cannot exceed the link bandwidth; two-way communication needs a complementary VL. The BAG is the minimum interval between two frames on a VL, from 1 to 128 ms:

Maximum bit rate of a VL
$$R_{max}=\frac{L_{max}\times8\times1000}{\text{BAG(ms)}}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\frac{1518\times8\times1000}{4}=3.036\times10^{6}\text{ bit/s}$$

Choosing a BAG: data recurring 100 times a second needs a BAG of at most 8 ms because 125 Hz exceeds 100 Hz.

📖 Explanation for beginners

Tooley Chapter 16 Q5 asks for the largest BAG for 400 messages a second; by Table 16.1 and the rule above the answer is 2 ms (500 Hz), not 4 ms (250 Hz) as the printed key says.

An AFDX frame is 64 to 1,518 bytes with a sequence number 0 to 255 and a 4-byte CRC; networks A and B back each other up

(Tooley 16.9.) The frame follows the IEEE 802.3 frame. Fields (bytes): preamble 7 and SFD 1 (not counted in the frame length), MAC destination 6, MAC source 6, Type 2 (always 0x0800 = IPv4), IP header 20, UDP 8, AFDX payload, padding (0 to 16), sequence number SN 1, FCS 4 (a CRC); then a 12-byte inter-frame gap.

Frame length
$$L=6+6+2+20+8+\max(P,17)+1+4\quad\Rightarrow\quad P=1\text{ to }1471:\;L=64\text{ to }1518\text{ bytes}$$

The SN starts at 0 at start-up, increments to 255 and wraps to 1. Integrity checking checks the SNs are in strict order; redundancy management drops replicas. Network B (blue) carries a copy of network A (red): protection against cable faults, connectors, switches, noise and RFI.

📖 Explanation for beginners

The site checked the frame arithmetic two ways: a 1-byte payload needs 16 bytes of padding (64 bytes in all), a 1,471-byte payload none (1518 bytes). The longest frame takes 1.2144 ms at 10 Mbit/s and 121.44 μs at 100 Mbit/s (frame only); the 12-byte gap lasts 0.96 μs at 100 Mbit/s.

PART 3/3
03

IMA gathers many avionic applications onto one shared computing platform joined by an AFDX network, each in its own partition

  • Why IMA, and what it is
  • IMA architecture versus federated
  • The IMA platform: RTOS, partitions, ARINC 653

IMA replaces one computer per system with one shared computing platform to cut cabling, weight and cost

(Tooley 16.1, 18.1, 18.2.) Each avionic system used to have its own computer, often in an LRU: many computers, much cabling, heavy and power-hungry. IMA is a concept (not one specific technology): many applications run on a shared computing platform joined by a high-speed, high-integrity multiplexed network. Three elements: a high-integrity common computing resource; a shared dual-redundant network (ARINC 664/AFDX, multicast, intelligent switches); a distributed architecture of centrally computed software applications and remotely located end systems. Example applications: air conditioning and cabin pressurisation, energy, fuel, pneumatics and hydraulics, brakes and landing gear, ice and rain protection, proximity sensing, fire detection.

📖 Explanation for beginners

Benefits (Tooley 18.1): manage more functions, lighter cabling (saving fuel and cost), support for new functions such as aircraft condition monitoring, multi-source support, fewer spares.

Compared with federated avionics, IMA centralises processing, uses one common backbone and isolates software by logical partitions

FeatureFederatedIMA
Processingmany embedded computers dedicated to separate functionscentralised on a shared IMA platform with partitioned applications
Data interconnection and cablingmany low and medium speed buses (ARINC 429, 629)one common high-speed switched backbone (ARINC 664, AFDX)
Application softwarephysically firewalled, on individual LRUslogically firewalled, several applications on one platform
Suppliersrestricted to a few sourcesideally open (multiple sources)

Airbus A380: semi-open (one hardware-cabinet supplier, a shared operating system); the Boeing 777 uses Honeywell's AIMS so it is closed; smaller Bombardier and Embraer aircraft are typically closed (Tooley 18.2.1, 18.3).

📖 Explanation for beginners

Each LRU uses an intelligent end system to connect to the nearest switch so it needs no individual cable back to the IMA platform. On the A380 the CPIOM modules (computing and I/O) host applications; the IOM modules only convert non-AFDX data to AFDX.

The IMA platform runs an RTOS that gives each application a memory partition and a time slice, following ARINC 653

(Tooley 18.4, 18.5.) The RTOS manages partitioning and scheduling. An application executive (APEX) allocates memory and time, manages processes, partition communication and error handling. Application software is not part of the core operating system so it can be validated and certified independently. The leading standard is ARINC 653, which must stay compatible with LRUs on ARINC 629 and ARINC 429 systems. Landing-gear example: the software sits on the IMA platform, proximity sensors reach it through data concentrators (PSDC, RDC) and AFDX switches; the ARINC 664 network has about a thousand times the bandwidth of the ARINC 429 bus it replaces.

📖 Explanation for beginners

Critical applications such as flight and engine control may stay on dedicated computers in a federated architecture: not everything has to move to the IMA platform.

Five worked examples

Example · Example 1: maximum bit rate of a VL (Tooley)

Frame 1,518 bytes, BAG 4 ms: $1518\times8\times1000/4=3{,}036{,}000$ bit/s ≈ 3 Mbit/s.

Result (checked by program): 3,036,000 bit/s

Example · Example 2: choosing a BAG for 400 messages a second

The BAG frequency must be ≥ 400 Hz: 500 Hz is 2 ms; 250 Hz (4 ms) is not enough.

Result (checked by program): 2 ms

Example · Example 3: bit rate for 256-byte frames and a 128 ms BAG (Tooley Chapter 16 Q6)

$256\times8\times1000/128=16{,}000$ bit/s.

Result (checked by program): 16,000 bit/s

Minimum: $6+6+2+20+8+17+1+4=64$ bytes; maximum with a 1,471-byte payload: 1,518 bytes.

Points to note in the sources
  • Tooley Chapter 16 Q5: printed key (b) 4 ms; by Table 16.1 and the book's own rule (data 100 times a second needs a BAG of at most 8 ms because 125 Hz exceeds 100 Hz) 400 times a second needs a BAG frequency of at least 400 Hz: 2 ms (500 Hz). The site uses (a).
  • Tooley Chapter 16 Q9: printed key (b) IP layer; but Q11 (key a) gives the transmit order UDP, IP, link layer so the ethernet header is added by the link layer. The site uses (c).
  • Key A.16 has 20 entries but the book prints only 16 multiple-choice questions in 16.11; the last four entries (17 c, 18 a, 19 b, 20 b) have no matching question.
  • Tooley 16.9 says the AFDX payload is 1 to 1,471 bytes while a key-point box says "17 to 1,471, below 17 padding is added": the two statements agree once payload and padding are combined (at least 17 bytes).
  • Tooley Test Your Understanding 16.8 asks for the inter-frame gap at 100 Mbit/s and the transmit time of the longest frame at 10 Mbit/s: the site computes 0.96 μs (12 bytes) and 1.2144 ms (frame only; 1.2304 ms with the preamble and gap).

Source errors and doubts

  • Tooley Chapter 16 Q5: printed key (b) 4 ms; by Table 16.1 and the book's own rule (data 100 times a second needs a BAG of at most 8 ms because 125 Hz exceeds 100 Hz) 400 times a second needs a BAG frequency of at least 400 Hz, i.e. 2 ms (500 Hz); 4 ms gives only 250 Hz. The site uses (a).
  • Tooley Chapter 16 Q9: printed key (b) "IP layer"; but Q11 (key a) gives the transmit order UDP, then IP, then the link layer, so the ethernet header is added by the link layer. The site uses (c).
  • Key A.16 has 20 entries but the book prints only 16 multiple-choice questions in 16.11; the last four entries (17 c, 18 a, 19 b, 20 b) have no matching question.
  • Tooley 16.9 says the AFDX payload is 1 to 1,471 bytes while the Key Point box says 17 to 1,471 with padding below 17: consistent once payload and padding are combined (at least 17 bytes). The total frame of 64 to 1,518 bytes was checked by adding the fields.
  • Tooley 18.5 says the ARINC 664 network has "one thousand times the bandwidth" of ARINC 429: right when comparing 100 Mbit/s with the 100 kbit/s of high-speed ARINC 429 (the site computes 1,000 times); the book gives no specific rates so this comparison is the site's.
  • Test Your Understanding 16.9 item 4 ("bit rate increases with BAG but decreases with frame length") is the reverse of the book's own formula ($R=L\times8\times1000/\text{BAG}$: it grows with L and falls with BAG); it is a true/false question so item 4 is either a deliberately false statement or a misprint; the site does not put it in the bank.
