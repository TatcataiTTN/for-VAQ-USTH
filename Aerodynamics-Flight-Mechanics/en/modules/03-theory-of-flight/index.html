MODULE 03 · Theory of Flight

Theory of Flight: the four forces, load factor, stall, high-speed flow and lift augmentation

This module uses the aerodynamic coefficients of module 02 to describe a real flight. First the balance of the four forces, glide ratio and the polar; then load factor and the stall in turns; then high-speed flow with Mach number, shock waves and sweepback. The last part covers the lift-augmentation devices (flaps, leading-edge devices, vortex generators) that a maintenance technician meets every day. The problems come from Anderson ch. 5, 8 and 11.

EASA Module 08, Sub-module 03 (pp. 3.1–3.20) · Anderson ch. 5, 8, 11 · Etkin & Reid ch. 3 sec. 3.1 and 3.3

PART 1/5
01

The four forces and steady flight

  • Lift balances weight and thrust balances drag in steady level flight
  • Glide ratio is the distance travelled per unit of height lost with the engine off
  • The polar curve shows how C_L and C_D are related
PART 1/5 · 03

Force balance: L = W, T = D

  • Lift acts perpendicular to the relative wind and upwards (EASA Q3-1)
  • To overcome drag and move the aircraft forward, thrust is needed (EASA Q3-2)
  • Steady level flight: L = W and T = D; any imbalance makes the aircraft climb, descend, accelerate or decelerate
  • Piper Cherokee wing (Anderson 5.4): W = 2450 lb, S = 170 ft², 120 mi/h at sea level needs C_L = 0.391
In plain words
Steady level flight is like standing still on a scale: the upward force equals the downward force, and the forward force equals the backward force.
PART 1/5 · 03

Glide ratio and the polar curve

  • Glide ratio = forward distance / height lost; travelling 10 000 ft forward while losing 1000 ft is 10 to 1 (EASA)
  • In a steady glide the glide ratio equals L/D = C_L/C_D
  • The polar plots C_L against C_D; the tangent from the origin gives the maximum L/D, the shallowest glide angle
  • The speed for best glide range differs from the speed for minimum sink
In plain words
A headwind shortens the ground range even though the glide ratio through the air is unchanged.
PART 2/5
02

Turns, load factor and the stall

  • In a level turn L cos φ = W so the load factor is n = 1/cos φ
  • Stall speed increases as √n
  • The stall occurs at a critical angle of attack, not at one fixed speed
PART 2/5 · 03

Forces in a turn

  • When banked by φ the vertical component of lift is L cos φ; to hold altitude lift must grow so that L cos φ = W
  • Load factor n = L/W = 1/cos φ, measured in g
  • Stall speed in the turn: V_s,turn = V_s √n

In plain words
At a 60° bank the load factor is 2 g and the stall speed rises 41%. An aircraft that stalls at 60 kt in straight flight stalls at about 85 kt in that turn.
PART 2/5 · 03

Stalls and the flight envelope

  • A stall occurs when the angle of attack exceeds the critical value: flow separates from the upper surface and C_L falls
  • Stall speed depends on weight, load factor and density, so an aircraft can stall at a seemingly safe speed (EASA p. 3.7)
  • On swept or tapered wings the stall tends to start at the tip; stall strips at the wing root make the stall start at the root (EASA)
In plain words
The "flight envelope" is the permitted region of speed and load factor. Outside it lies stall or structural failure.

  • Beyond 60° both curves climb very steeply
PART 3/5
03

Load factor and structural limits

  • Load factor is measured in g: 3 g means total load three times the weight
  • The limit load of the Normal category is +3.8 and −1.52 g
  • A safety factor of 1.5 applied to the limit load gives the ultimate load
PART 3/5 · 03

In plain words
For a technician: after severe turbulence or a hard landing the structure must be inspected, because it may have exceeded the limit load even though it looks normal outside.
PART 4/5
04

High-speed flow, Mach number and shock waves

  • Mach number is the ratio of true airspeed to the speed of sound
  • The speed of sound depends on temperature: a = √(γRT)
  • The critical Mach number is the free-stream M at which some point on the wing reaches sonic speed
PART 4/5 · 03

Speed of sound and Mach number

  • At 15 °C sea level a = 661 kt (EASA gives 661 kt); at 40 000 ft ISA temperature is −56.5 °C so a = 574 kt (EASA gives 574 kt)
  • Anderson 8.1: at 230 K, a = 304.0 m/s
  • The same Mach number corresponds to a lower true airspeed at altitude because the air is colder
  • Up to about 260 kt air can be treated as incompressible (EASA)
In plain words
At the same M 0.8, the true airspeed is lower at altitude than near sea level, because a is smaller there.
PART 4/5 · 03

Critical Mach, shock waves and compressibility

  • Air over the wing can move at up to twice the aircraft speed, so sonic flow can appear locally while the aircraft is still subsonic (EASA)
  • Anderson 11.4: with C_p,0 = −0.41 the Prandtl-Glauert rule gives M_cr ≈ 0.744
  • Across a normal shock at M₁ = 2.6: M₂ = 0.504, p₂/p₁ = 7.72, ρ₂/ρ₁ = 3.449, T₂/T₁ = 2.238 (Anderson 8.7)
  • Compressibility brings wave drag, buffeting and control difficulty; EASA answer Q3-6 is "compressibility"
In plain words
A normal shock always takes the flow from supersonic to subsonic, while pressure, temperature and density jump abruptly.

  • At M = 1.05 a bow wave forms ahead of the airfoil
PART 4/5 · 03

Original figure: the sweepback effect

  • True speed M 0.85 but the component normal to the leading edge is only M 0.70; with M_n = M cos Λ this means Λ = 34.6° (worked out from the two numbers on the figure; the book does not print this angle)
  • Spanwise flow is the cause of tip stall on swept wings
PART 5/5
05

Sweepback and lift augmentation

  • Sweepback delays the onset of compressibility effects
  • Flaps on the trailing edge increase lift and drag; leading-edge devices keep the flow attached longer
  • Fixed devices such as vortex generators and stall fences manage the airflow
PART 5/5 · 03

Sweepback

  • Only the velocity component normal to the quarter-chord line produces the compressibility effect; sweeping the wing reduces it
  • Result: the critical Mach number rises and wave drag appears later (EASA Q3-6 "a delay in the onset of compressibility effects")
  • In return the lift-curve slope falls and tip stall becomes more likely
In plain words
Like wading diagonally across a stream rather than straight against it: the water "sees" a smaller speed.

History

Reaching the sound barrier and the swept wing

In the 1940s, as aircraft approached the speed of sound, engineers ran into soaring drag, buffeting and loss of control, often called the "sound barrier". The remedy was the swept wing, proposed by Adolf Busemann at the 1935 Volta conference and later used on most jet aircraft. Anderson devotes Historical Note sections of chapters 9 and 11 to this history. EASA only summarises the result: sweepback delays the onset of compressibility effects.

Case

Case: a Piper Cherokee wing at 120 mi/h

  • Anderson 5.4. The wing has S = 170 ft² and b = 32 ft, so AR = 6.02. Flying at 120 mi/h = 176 ft/s at sea level with W = 2450 lb gives q = 36.81 lb/ft² and C_L = 0.391. The finite-wing lift slope is a = 0.0765 per degree, so the geometric angle of attack is α = C_L/a + α_L=0 = 2.12°.
  • Anderson 5.5. With e = 0.64 the induced-drag coefficient is C_Di = 0.0127 and the induced drag is 79.2 lb.
Pitfalls

Common pitfalls

  • EASA defines load factor as the ratio of the maximum load the aircraft can sustain to its weight. In a real turn you use n = L/W. Both are measured in g, but one is a design limit and the other is the value occurring.
  • A stall depends on angle of attack, not on one fixed speed.
  • EASA gives the temperature at 40 000 ft as −55 °C, while the ISA gives −56.5 °C. The difference is rounding, not an error.
  • The critical Mach number is not the speed of sound: at M_cr the aircraft is still subsonic.
  • Anderson 5.4 uses the finite-wing slope a = a₀/[1 + 57.3a₀(1+τ)/(πAR)], not the airfoil's a₀.

5.4 – 5.5. A Piper Cherokee has S = 170 ft², b = 32 ft, W = 2450 lb and flies at 120 mi/h at sea level. The airfoil has a₀ = 0.1033/degree, α_L=0 = −3°, τ = 0.12. Find the geometric angle of attack; then find the induced drag with e = 0.64.

Anderson 6th, Problems 5.4 and 5.5

Show the solution

What the problem says. A Piper Cherokee has S = 170 ft², b = 32 ft and W = 2450 lb, and flies level at 120 mi/h at sea level. The airfoil has a₀ = 0.1033/degree, α_L=0 = −3°, τ = 0.12. Find the geometric angle of attack, then the induced drag with e = 0.64.

Idea. Level flight means L = W, which gives C_L. With C_L you find the angle of attack through the finite-wing lift slope (not the airfoil's), and C_D,i from C_L²/(π e AR).

Steps.

  1. AR = b²/S = 32²/170 = 6.024; V = 120 mi/h = 176 ft/s; q = ½ × 0.002377 × 176² = 36.81 lb/ft².
  2. L = W ⇒ C_L = W/(qS) = 2450/(36.81 × 170) = 0.3915.
  3. Finite-wing slope: a = a₀/[1 + 57.3a₀(1 + τ)/(πAR)] = 0.0765/degree.
  4. α = C_L/a + α_L=0 = 5.117 − 3 = 2.12°.
  5. C_D,i = C_L²/(π e AR) = 0.3915²/(π × 0.64 × 6.024) = 0.01265; D_i = C_D,i q S = 79.2 lb.

Check: Using a₀ instead of a gives 0.79°, which is wrong because a finite wing has a smaller slope than its airfoil. The induced drag of 79 lb is small against the 2450 lb weight, which is plausible for a small aircraft at this speed. Problems 5.4 and 5.5 are not in the 4th-edition Solution Manual so they could not be compared; the results were rechecked by an independent calculation in the notebook.

8.1, 8.11. (8.1) Find the speed of sound at 230 K. (8.11) A flow at 1 atm and 288 K; a Pitot tube reads 1.555 atm. Find the flow velocity.

Anderson 6th, Problems 8.1 and 8.11

Show the solution

What the problem says. (8.1) Find the speed of sound in air at 230 K. (8.11) A flow at 1 atm and 288 K; a Pitot tube reads 1.555 atm. Find the flow velocity.

Idea. (8.1) Use a = √(γRT). (8.11) The Pitot tube reads the total pressure p₀. For compressible subsonic flow, p₀/p = (1 + 0.2M²)^3.5. Solve for M, then V = M·a with a evaluated at 288 K.

Steps.

  1. (8.1) a = √(γRT) = √(1.4 × 287 × 230) = √92 414 = 304.0 m/s.
  2. (8.11) p₀/p = 1.555 = (1 + 0.2M²)^3.5, so 1 + 0.2M² = 1.555^(1/3.5) = 1.1344 and M = 0.8199.
  3. a = √(1.4 × 287 × 288) = 340.2 m/s, so V = M a = 0.8199 × 340.2 = 278.9 m/s.

Check: The Solution Manual gives the same result. Incompressible Bernoulli, V = √(2Δp/ρ) = 303.0 m/s, would be about 9% too high: at M ≈ 0.8 compressibility cannot be ignored.

8.7, 11.4. (8.7) A normal shock with M₁ = 2.6, p₁ = 1 atm, T₁ = 288 K: find M₂, p₂, T₂. (11.4) The minimum pressure coefficient of an airfoil in incompressible flow is C_p,0 = −0.41: estimate M_cr.

Anderson 6th, Problems 8.7 and 11.4

Show the solution

What the problem says. (8.7) A normal shock has M₁ = 2.6, p₁ = 1 atm, T₁ = 288 K. Find M₂, p₂, T₂. (11.4) In incompressible flow the minimum pressure coefficient of an airfoil is C_p,0 = −0.41. Estimate the critical Mach number with the Prandtl-Glauert rule.

Idea. (8.7) Use the normal-shock relations directly at M₁ = 2.6. (11.4) M_cr is the M∞ at which the local Mach number at the point of strongest suction reaches 1: the intersection of the Prandtl-Glauert curve C_p,0/√(1 − M²) with the curve C_p,cr(M).

Steps.

  1. (8.7) M₂² = [(γ−1)M₁² + 2]/[2γM₁² − (γ−1)] = 0.2539 ⇒ M₂ = 0.504.
  2. p₂/p₁ = 1 + 2γ(M₁² − 1)/(γ + 1) = 7.720 ⇒ p₂ = 7.72 atm.
  3. T₂/T₁ = 2.238 ⇒ T₂ = 2.238 × 288 = 644.6 K.
  4. (11.4) The pressure coefficient where the local M = 1: C_p,cr(M) = (2/γM²)[((1 + 0.2M²)/1.2)^3.5 − 1].
  5. Solving −0.41/√(1 − M²) = C_p,cr(M) numerically (bisection): M_cr = 0.744.

Check: Problem 8.7 agrees with the Solution Manual; M₂ < 1 as for every normal shock and the temperature rises by a factor of nearly 2.2. Problem 11.4: the Solution Manual plots the curves and reads M_cr ≈ 0.74, which agrees with 0.744.
