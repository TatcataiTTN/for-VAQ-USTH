MODULE 09 · Potential flow

Elementary potential flows: source, vortex, doublet and flow past a cylinder

Module 02 used Bernoulli and the lift coefficient as ready-made tools. This module shows where they come from: for inviscid, incompressible, irrotational flow the velocity is the gradient of a potential φ and there is a stream function ψ. Adding the elementary flows (uniform, source, vortex, doublet) gives flow past a cylinder with C_p = 1 − 4 sin²θ, zero drag (d'Alembert's paradox) and, with circulation Γ added, a lift L′ = ρVΓ. Every problem is solved in four steps: what the problem says, the idea, the steps, a check.

Anderson ch. 3 (sec. 3.9–3.16; Problems 3.15–3.22) · EASA Module 08, Sub-module 02 (p. 2.3)

PART 1/5
01

Stream function, potential and the elementary flows

  • Irrotational flow: V = ∇φ; incompressible flow: ∇·V = 0, so ∇²φ = 0 (Laplace's equation)
  • Lines of constant ψ are streamlines
  • Laplace's equation is linear, so simple solutions can be added

📖 Explained for complete beginners
A doublet is a source and a sink brought together with strength rising as the spacing shrinks; it is the "shrunken cylinder" for the next step.
PART 2/5
02

The non-lifting cylinder

  • Uniform flow + doublet gives ψ = V r sin θ (1 − R²/r²)
  • On the surface (r = R): V_θ = −2V sin θ and C_p = 1 − 4 sin²θ
  • Drag and lift are both zero (d'Alembert's paradox)

  • At θ = 0° and 180°: stagnation points, C_p = +1
  • At θ = 90°: maximum velocity 2V∞ and C_p = −3 (strongest suction)
  • The distribution is symmetric front-to-back so the force along the flow is zero
📖 Explained for complete beginners
A real cylinder has large drag because the boundary layer separates behind it (module 02): inviscid theory cannot see that.
PART 3/5
03

The lifting cylinder and the Kutta-Joukowski theorem

  • Add a vortex Γ at the centre: V_θ(surface) = −2V sin θ − Γ/(2πR)
  • The stagnation points move down: sin θ = −Γ/(4πVR)
  • Lift per unit span L′ = ρ V Γ, valid for any two-dimensional body
PART 3/5 · 09

Example: Γ/(4πVR) = 0.5

  • sin θ_s = −0.5 so the stagnation points are at θ = -30° and 210°, both below the horizontal axis
  • The maximum surface velocity occurs at the top (θ = 90°): |V_θ| = 2V + V = 3.0 V∞ (at the bottom only 2V − V = V∞ remains), so the upper surface has lower pressure: that is the lift
  • When Γ/(4πVR) = 1 the two stagnation points merge at the bottom; beyond that the stagnation point leaves the surface
📖 Explained for complete beginners
Faster flow on one side means lower pressure on that side: this is the mechanism of lift (module 02) and of the Magnus effect.
PART 4/5
04

Applications: the compressibility limit and the drag of wires

  • Local velocity on a cylinder reaches 2V∞, so compressibility appears long before the free-stream limit
  • The drag of a cylinder at high Re is D = q S C_D with C_D from experiment
  • Potential theory gives no drag: experimental data are needed
PART 4/5 · 09

Problems 3.19 and 3.22

  • 3.19: q = ½ρV² = 36.8 lb/ft² at 120 mi/h; 25 ft of 2 in struts: D = 153 lb; 80 ft of 3/32 in wires: D = 23 lb; total 176 lb, which is 58% of the zero-lift drag 305 lb (the Solution Manual uses C_D = 1 read from a chart)
  • 3.22: 250 mi/h = 111.76 m/s is the maximum local velocity; since V_max = 2V∞, V∞ = 55.88 m/s ≈ 125 mi/h; the local Mach number is then 0.33
📖 Explained for complete beginners
This is why biplanes with bracing wires were slow: the struts and wires are over half the zero-lift drag at 120 mi/h; Anderson notes that designers in the 1930s moved to internally braced wings.

📜 history

Bernoulli, Euler, Kutta and Joukowski

Anderson devotes Historical Notes 3.19 and 4.15 to Bernoulli and Euler (the origins of fluid theory) and to Kutta and Joukowski. Martin Kutta (1902) and Nikolai Joukowski (1906) independently reached L′ = ρVΓ; Joukowski recognised that lift is proportional to the circulation round the wing.

🔎 case

Case: the spinning cylinder

A spinning cylinder in a 30 m/s stream at sea level (ρ = 1.23 kg/m³) feels a lift of 6 N per metre of span. From L′ = ρVΓ the circulation is Γ = 6/(1.23 × 30) = 0.163 m²/s (Problem 3.18). This is the Magnus effect: a spinning ball curves, and Flettner ships use rotors instead of sails.

⚠️ watch out

Common pitfalls

(1) A wing's lift does not come from the wing "spinning": circulation is a mathematical tool fixed by the Kutta condition. (2) An inviscid cylinder has zero drag but a real one has large drag: that is flow separation. (3) The sign of V_θ depends on convention: in the book a clockwise vortex has Γ > 0. (4) If V∞ doubles with Γ unchanged the streamline shape changes; without circulation the shape stays the same (Problems 3.16, 3.17). (5) Problem 3.19 depends on C_D = 1 read from a chart; that number is approximate.

3.15. Non-lifting flow round a cylinder: derive C_p at any point (r, θ) and show that on the surface it reduces to C_p = 1 − 4 sin²θ.

Anderson 6th, Problem 3.15

👁️ Show the solution

What the problem says. We need C_p everywhere, not only on the surface.

Idea. C_p = 1 − (V/V∞)², so we only need V² = V_r² + V_θ² from the stream function.

Steps.

  1. From ψ = V∞ r sin θ (1 − R²/r²): V_r = (1/r)∂ψ/∂θ = V∞ cos θ (1 − R²/r²).
  2. V_θ = −∂ψ/∂r = −V∞ sin θ (1 + R²/r²).
  3. V²/V∞² = (1 − R²/r²)² cos²θ + (1 + R²/r²)² sin²θ.
  4. C_p = 1 − (1 − R²/r²)² cos²θ − (1 + R²/r²)² sin²θ.
  5. On the surface r = R: the first term vanishes and (1 + 1)² = 4, so C_p = 1 − 4 sin²θ.

Check: The Solution Manual gives the same result. Numerical check: θ = 90° ⇒ C_p = −3; θ = 0° ⇒ +1.

3.16 – 3.17. If V∞ doubles, does the shape of the streamlines round a cylinder change: (a) without circulation; (b) with fixed circulation Γ?

Anderson 6th, Problems 3.16, 3.17

👁️ Show the solution

What the problem says. The shape of the streamlines is fixed by the direction of the velocity at each point.

Idea. Look at the ratio V_θ/V_r: if it does not change with V∞ the shape does not change.

Steps.

  1. (a) V_r = V∞ f(r, θ) and V_θ = V∞ g(r, θ), so V_θ/V_r = g/f is independent of V∞. The shape does not change.
  2. (b) V_θ = −(1 + R²/r²) V∞ sin θ − Γ/(2πr): the second term has no V∞.
  3. Hence V_θ/V_r depends on V∞. The shape changes.

Check: Example: the stagnation point sin θ = −Γ/(4πV∞R) moves when V∞ changes, confirming (b).

3.19. A biplane at 120 mi/h sea level: struts totalling 25 ft of 2 in diameter and bracing wires totalling 80 ft of 3/32 in diameter. Find their drag and compare it with the zero-lift drag (S = 230 ft², C_D,0 = 0.036).

Anderson 6th, Problem 3.19

👁️ Show the solution

What the problem says. They are cylinders so drag depends on the frontal area = length × diameter.

Idea. Use D = q S C_D; the Solution Manual takes C_D = 1 from a chart (Re about 2×10⁵ for the struts, 9×10³ for the wires).

Steps.

  1. V = 120 mi/h = 176 ft/s; q = ½ × 0.002377 × 176² = 36.8 lb/ft².
  2. Struts: S = 25 × (2/12) = 4.167 ft² ⇒ D = 36.8 × 4.167 × 1 = 153 lb.
  3. Wires: S = 80 × (3/32/12) = 0.625 ft² ⇒ D = 23 lb.
  4. Total 176 lb. Zero-lift drag of the whole aircraft D₀ = q S C_D,0 = 36.8 × 230 × 0.036 = 305 lb.
  5. Ratio = 176/305 = 58%.

Check: Solution Manual: 153 + 23 = 176 lb and 58%. The tiny difference is because it rounds 2 in to 0.167 ft.

3.21 – 3.22. (3.21) ψ₁ = 0 on the surface and ψ₂ passes through (1.2R, π/2); find the point over the top through which ψ₃ passes. (3.22) The wind-tunnel speed at which compressibility starts to matter when the local velocity exceeds 250 mi/h.

Anderson 6th, Problems 3.21, 3.22

👁️ Show the solution

What the problem says. (3.21) The streamlines are equally spaced upstream so ψ₃ = 2ψ₂. (3.22) Compressibility matters where the velocity is highest.

Idea. Use ψ = V r sin θ (1 − R²/r²) at θ = 90°; and V_max = 2V∞ on the cylinder.

Steps.

  1. (3.21) At θ = 90°, ψ/(VR) = x − 1/x with x = r/R. ψ₂/(VR) = 1.2 − 1/1.2 = 0.3667.
  2. ψ₃ = 2ψ₂ ⇒ x − 1/x = 0.7333 ⇒ x² − 0.7333x − 1 = 0 ⇒ x = 1.432.
  3. Gaps between the streamlines over the top: 0.2R then 0.232R: they are squeezed close to the body because the flow speeds up (continuity).
  4. (3.22) V_max = 2V∞ ⇒ V∞ = 250/2 = 125 mi/h = 55.9 m/s.

Check: Check (3.21): substituting x = 1.432: x − 1/x = 0.7333 = 2ψ₂ ✓. Check (3.22): local Mach number = 0.33.
