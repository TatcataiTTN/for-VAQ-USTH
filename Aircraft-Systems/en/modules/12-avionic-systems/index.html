12. Digital avionic systems: fly-by-wire, FMS, GPS, IRS, TCAS, test equipment

PART 1/3
01

Fly-by-wire replaces mechanical links with computers and wires; the FMS turns sensor data into automatic navigation

  • Fly-by-wire: principle, advantages and risks
  • FMS: four elements and the 28-day database

Fly-by-wire moves the control surfaces with electrical signals, so it needs redundant computers

With fly-by-wire (FBW) electrical wires drive motors, servos and actuators instead of mechanical linkages operating hydraulic actuators. Computers convert the pilot's commands into electrical signals. FBW was first introduced on a commercial passenger aircraft on the Airbus A320 in 1988 (Tooley 15.4).

AdvantagesRisks, drawbacks
less pilot workload, automatic control possiblelack of "feel"
lower weight, less fuel, less CO₂concern about reliability if computers or power fail
keeps the aircraft within the flight protection envelopeso redundant computers and usually a mechanical or hydraulic backup
families of similar aircraft (107-seat A318 to 555-seat A380): shorter training
📖 Explanation for beginners

Key point for the exam: a single failure must not change the operation of the system (Tooley Chapter 15 Q6, answer c), thanks to redundancy.

The FMS combines GPS, VOR/DME, IRS and dead reckoning into one navigation system with a database updated every 28 days

The FMS provides flight planning and en-route, terminal and nonprecision approach guidance: lateral navigation (LNAV), vertical navigation (VNAV), time and fuel predictions, departure procedures (DP) and standard terminal arrival routes (STAR). Four main elements: the FMC, a display system (could be the EFIS), the DBU (database storage) and the CDU with keypad (Tooley 15.5).

Navigation database update cycle
$$365\div28=13.04\;\Rightarrow\;13\text{ cycles a year}\quad(13\times28=364\text{ days})$$

Before flight the waypoints (up to 100 in some systems) are entered through the CDU and the aircraft weight and fuel load are updated. The FMS can be a dual fit so that the two systems cross-check the position.

📖 Explanation for beginners

Mind the two different kinds of data: the NAVIGATION database is updated every 28 days, while the FLIGHT data (weight, fuel) is entered before each flight. The printed key of Tooley Chapter 15 Q4 picks (b), the second kind.

PART 2/3
02

GPS positions with 24 satellites and atomic clocks; the IRS integrates acceleration so it needs no outside signal

  • GPS: three segments, orbits and frequencies
  • IRS: gyros, accelerometers, integration

GPS has 24 satellites at 20,200 km with a 12-hour period, and three segments: space, user and control

(Tooley 15.6.) Space segment: at least 24 satellites in 6 circular orbits (4 per plane), 20,200 km (10,900 nautical miles) high, inclined at 55°, 12-hour period; at least 6 satellites are always in view. User segment: receivers, processors, antennas. Control segment: a master station in Colorado Springs, 5 monitor stations, 3 ground antennas. Each satellite has an atomic clock (0.1 s in 10,000 years according to the book); the US can degrade accuracy by up to 1,000 m.

Check of the orbital period with Kepler's law
$$T=2\pi\sqrt{r^3/\mu},\quad r=6371+20200=26571\text{ km}\;\Rightarrow\;T\approx11.97\text{ h}$$
📖 Explanation for beginners

The site checked two ways: Kepler's law gives 11.97 hours, scaling from the Moon's orbit gives 11.92 hours; both agree with Tooley's 12 hours. 20,200 km divided by 1.852 is 10,907 nautical miles (the book rounds to 10,900).

Each satellite transmits carriers L1 and L2 modulated by pseudo-random codes: C/A at 1.023 MHz and P at 10.23 MHz

(Tooley 15.6.4.)

SignalFrequencyCode and repeat period
L11,575.42 MHzC/A code 1.023 MHz, repeats every 1 ms
L21,227.60 MHzP code 10.23 MHz, repeats every 267 days; a unique seven-day segment per satellite
Navigation datasuperimposed on each signalsatellite status, ephemeris, clock error, tropospheric and ionospheric data
Y codethe P code "dithered"military/political use only

Chips in one C/A period: $1.023\times10^6\times10^{-3}=1023$.

📖 Explanation for beginners

Do not confuse the carrier frequencies (1,575.42 and 1,227.60 MHz) with the code rates (1.023 and 10.23 MHz). The site checked these numbers on page images 252 and 253 of the book.

An IRS has gyros, accelerometers, a computer and a clock, and integrates acceleration to velocity and then distance

(Tooley 15.7.) An IRS/INS is a passive system needing no outside input, so it is hard to jam. Three gyros mounted orthogonally in a gimbal sense roll, pitch and heading; three accelerometers measure acceleration on three axes. The computer integrates at the clock rate:

Double integration
$$v=\int a\,dt,\qquad s=\int v\,dt\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;a=\text{const}:\;v=at,\;s=\tfrac12at^2$$

Example: $a=2$ m/s² for 30 s gives $v=60$ m/s and $s=900$ m. Two kinds: gimballed (a platform holding a fixed orientation, low error but liable to "topple" in violent manoeuvres) and strap-down (fixed to the airframe, measuring angular rate, using laser or optical gyros with few moving parts).

📖 Explanation for beginners

A small accelerometer error accumulates: 0.01 m/s² for 40 s gives a distance error of 8 m. That is why initialisation (alignment) before flight is crucial. The site computed the integrals by formula and by numerical summation: they agree.

PART 3/3
03

TCAS uses the closure rate to warn of collisions vertically; ATE and BITE help locate faults

  • TCAS: TA, RA and time to the closest point
  • Automatic test equipment and built-in test

TCAS computes the time to the closest point of approach as distance over closure rate, then issues TA and RA

(Tooley 15.8.) TCAS is a secondary radar: it interrogates and receives from other TCAS-equipped aircraft. The most important variable is the closure rate; bearing and heading are not needed.

Time to the closest point of approach
$$\tau=\frac{d}{v_{closure}}\qquad(10\text{ NM},\;600\text{ kt}\Rightarrow\tau=60\text{ s})$$
AlertShown on the NDMeaning
TA (traffic advisory)the word TRAFFIC in yellow, a yellow circle, "traffic, traffic"lower level: only to note a potential conflict
RA (resolution advisory)a red square, aural and visual pitch commandshighest level: disengage the autopilot and fly the command manually; vertical sense only
📖 Explanation for beginners

If the other aircraft reports no altitude TCAS assumes it is within 1,200 ft and will not issue an RA. TCAS also needs the IRS, gear sensors, the radio altimeter and the GPWS (the GPWS has priority).

ATE is expensive ground equipment for LRUs, while BITE is a built-in self-test that flags faults along the signal flow

ATE (Tooley 15.9): a dedicated ground test instrument making many simultaneous connections to the equipment under test (an LRU or printed circuit card) to gather data very fast; usually dedicated to one avionic system and expensive, so used only by original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) and licensed repairers. BITE (15.10): an integral self-test, usually of the signal-flow type; if the flow is interrupted or leaves the accepted levels a warning is given. Capabilities: real-time monitoring, continuous display, module failure isolation, status verification, go/no-go alarms, quantitative displays, degraded-operation status, percentage of deterioration.

📖 Explanation for beginners

Example on Airbus: the crew records an ECAM failure message and maintenance staff then use the cockpit maintenance panel and the BITE on each computer in the avionics bay. The central maintenance computer monitors and tests both on the ground and in flight (Chapter 15 Q8).
