MODULE 07 · Oblique shocks

Oblique shocks, Prandtl-Meyer expansion waves and the supersonic airfoil

When a supersonic flow is turned through an angle it forms an oblique shock (compression) or a Prandtl-Meyer expansion fan. This module solves the Anderson ch. 9 problems: Mach angle, the θ-β-M relation, maximum deflection and detached shocks, total-pressure loss in multi-wave systems (supersonic inlets), the maximum Prandtl-Meyer turn of 130.45° from sonic flow, and shock-expansion theory for a flat plate at M = 2.6. Every number is computed directly, with no tables.

Anderson ch. 9 (Problems 9.1, 9.2, 9.4, 9.5, 9.6, 9.8, 9.13, 9.15, 9.20) · EASA Module 08, Sub-module 03 (p. 3.13, Figure 3-11)

PART 1/5
01

Mach waves and oblique shocks

  • A small disturbance in supersonic flow makes a Mach wave at angle μ = arcsin(1/M)
  • A finite disturbance makes an oblique shock at β > μ
  • An oblique shock is a normal shock for the normal component M₁ sin β

PART 2/5
02

The θ-β-M relation and maximum deflection

  • tan θ = 2cot β·(M₁²sin²β − 1)/[M₁²(γ + cos 2β) + 2]
  • For a given M₁ there is a maximum deflection θ_max; beyond it the shock detaches and curves ahead of the body
  • Every θ < θ_max has two solutions β: the weak (usual) and the strong shock
PART 2/5 · 07

Problems 9.5 and 9.6

  • 9.5: 22.2° half-angle wedge, M₁ = 2.5: β = 45.89°, p₂ = 3.59 atm, T₂ = 458 K, M₂ = 1.537
  • 9.6: at M = 2.4, θ_max = 28.68° so the plate can reach this angle of attack with the shock still attached; the maximum surface pressure is p₂/p₁ = 5.32
  • β is the numerical root of a trigonometric equation, found by bisection in this module
📖 Explained for complete beginners
A 22.2° deflection at M 2.5 is fairly close to θ_max (≈ 29.8°) so the shock is quite strong; more would detach it.
PART 3/5
03

Total-pressure loss and the supersonic inlet

  • Slowing a supersonic flow with several oblique shocks and then a weak normal shock loses less p₀ than one strong normal shock
  • p₀,₂/p₀,₁ works for an oblique shock with M_n1; p₀,₂/p₁ does not (Problems 9.3, 9.4)
  • This is the principle of the supersonic engine inlet

PART 4/5
04

Prandtl-Meyer expansion waves

  • Supersonic flow turning round a convex corner expands through a fan of Mach waves with no loss in p₀
  • The Prandtl-Meyer angle ν(M): θ = ν(M₂) − ν(M₁)
  • The maximum turn from sonic flow is ν(∞) = 130.45°
PART 4/5 · 07

Problems 9.15 and 9.20

  • 9.15: ν(∞) = (√((γ+1)/(γ−1)) − 1)·90° = 130.45°
  • 9.20: M₁ = 3, p₁ = 1 atm, a 20° expansion then a 20° compression: after the expansion M = 4.318, p/p₁ = 0.160; after the compression M₃ = 2.725, p₃ = 0.918 atm
  • p₃ ≠ p₁ although the flow direction is the same: the compression shock loses p₀ while the expansion does not
📖 Explained for complete beginners
Problem 9.20 asks "why": because the shock is irreversible and entropy rises, so the original state cannot be recovered.
PART 5/5
05

The supersonic flat plate: shock-expansion theory

  • The lower surface gets an oblique shock (pressure up), the upper surface an expansion (pressure down)
  • Lift c_l = (p_l − p_u)cos α/q and wave drag c_d,w = (p_l − p_u)sin α/q
  • Ackeret's linear theory: c_l = 4α/√(M² − 1)

📜 history

Mach, Prandtl, Meyer and Ackeret

Ernst Mach photographed a supersonic bullet with its shock waves in 1887; Anderson devotes a Historical Note (9.11) to him. Theodor Meyer, a student of Prandtl, gave the expansion-wave solution in his 1908 dissertation, hence the name Prandtl-Meyer. Jakob Ackeret published the linear theory for supersonic airfoils in 1925. The NACA compressible-flow tables TR-1135 (1953) collect these results.

🔎 case

Case: slowing an M = 4 flow at an inlet

A hypothetical inlet receives M = 4 flow. With one normal shock only 13.9% of the total pressure remains. Adding a 25.3° oblique shock ahead raises it to 31.4%, and a further 20° oblique shock to 42.0% (Anderson 9.8). That is why supersonic aircraft inlets have a multi-step cone or ramp. The numbers are the book problem's, not data of a specific inlet.

⚠️ watch out

Common pitfalls

(1) The Mach number behind an oblique shock is M₂ = M_n2/sin(β − θ), not M_n2. (2) Choose the correct β root (weak or strong); the weak one is the usual. (3) The p₀,₂/p₁ table column applies only to a normal shock so it cannot be used with the M_n1 of an oblique shock to find p₀,₂ (Problems 9.3, 9.4). (4) After an expansion and an equal compression the pressure does not return to the original because the compression shock loses p₀ (Problem 9.20). (5) Ackeret theory is for small angles only.
