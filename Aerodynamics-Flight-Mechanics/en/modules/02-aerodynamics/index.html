MODULE 02 · Aerodynamics

Aerodynamics: airflow, boundary layer, wings and force coefficients

This module explains why a wing produces lift and drag. It starts from relative motion and Newton's laws, moves through Bernoulli's principle and the boundary layer, to wing geometry (chord, camber, aspect ratio, mean aerodynamic chord) and finally the coefficients C_L, C_D, the polar curve and the centre of pressure, with real Anderson problems on the venturi, the Pitot tube, the NACA 2412 airfoil and thin-airfoil theory.

EASA Module 08, Sub-module 02 (pp. 2.1–2.20) · Anderson ch. 1, 3, 4, 5 · Etkin & Reid ch. 2 sec. 2.2 and Appendix C

PART 1/5
01

Relative motion and Newton's laws

  • The force on a body is the same whether it moves through still air or air moves past it
  • Speed differs from velocity: velocity has a direction
  • Newton's three laws govern the action of air on a wing
PART 1/5 · 02

Velocity, acceleration and the free stream

  • Speed is the rate of motion in relation to time; velocity is speed with a direction
  • Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity (positive when speeding up, negative when slowing)
  • The free stream is air not yet disturbed by the aircraft; its velocity equals the aircraft speed and its pressure is the static pressure
  • At the stagnation point ahead of the wing the velocity is zero
📖 Explained for complete beginners
An aircraft flying at 260 km/h does not tell where it goes; a velocity of 260 km/h to the south-west does. In aerodynamics the direction of the flow relative to the wing decides the force.
PART 1/5 · 02

Newton's three laws in flight

  • Law 1 (inertia): a body at rest or in uniform straight motion keeps its state unless acted on by an external force
  • Law 2: the change of motion is proportional to the force and in the direction of the force (F = ma)
  • Law 3: every force has an equal and opposite reaction; the wing pushes air down and the air pushes the wing up
📖 Explained for complete beginners
EASA answer 3-3: a body keeps its state "until acted on by some other force". That is Newton's first law.
PART 2/5
02

Bernoulli's principle and subsonic flow

  • Fluid flowing through a constriction speeds up and its pressure drops
  • The curved wing surface acts on the flow like the constriction in a venturi
  • The pressure difference between the upper and lower surfaces is lift
PART 2/5 · 02

Bernoulli: p + ½ρV² = constant

  • For inviscid, incompressible flow, static pressure p plus dynamic pressure q = ½ρV² equals the total pressure p₀ along a streamline
  • Streamlines close together mean high velocity and low pressure; far apart means low velocity
  • The stagnation point has the total pressure, larger than atmospheric by the dynamic pressure
  • A Pitot tube measures total pressure, from which the speed follows
📖 Explained for complete beginners
The flow's energy has a "squeeze" part (p) and a "motion" part (½ρV²). If the flow speeds up, the squeeze part must fall.
PART 2/5 · 02

Venturi, Pitot tube and wind tunnel

  • A wind tunnel with area ratio 12 and a 10 cm mercury pressure difference: V = 147.8 m/s (Anderson 3.4)
  • A Pitot tube on an aircraft reads 1.07×10⁵ N/m² at sea level: V = 98.8 m/s (Anderson 3.6)
  • A venturi with a 0.85 throat area ratio in a 90 m/s stream creates a maximum vacuum of 1913 N/m² (Anderson 3.3)
  • Bernoulli holds only while the flow stays attached; once it separates it no longer applies (EASA)
📖 Explained for complete beginners
All three examples use the same equation; only what is known and what is unknown change.
PART 3/5
03

Boundary layer and friction

  • The boundary layer is the air closest to the aircraft surface
  • Because of viscosity, velocity at the surface is nearly zero
  • A laminar layer becomes turbulent and may then separate from the surface
PART 3/5 · 02

Laminar, turbulent and separated flow

  • Laminar boundary layer: smooth sliding layers, low friction but easily separated
  • Turbulent boundary layer: thicker, faster, more friction but clings better to the surface
  • Flow that separates from the wing makes turbulence that produces no lift; this is the origin of the stall
  • Skin friction is the drag due to viscosity within the boundary layer
📖 Explained for complete beginners
A golf ball has dimples to make the boundary layer turbulent on purpose, so the flow clings longer behind the ball and total drag falls.
PART 4/5
04

Wing geometry and terminology

  • Airfoil: chord, camber, thickness
  • Aspect ratio = span / chord
  • Wing-tip vortices come from the pressure difference between the two wing surfaces
PART 4/5 · 02

Wing terms in the syllabus

TermMeaning
Chorddistance from leading to trailing edge (EASA 2-4 calls it the width of the wing)
Cambercurvature of the mean line of the airfoil
Aspect ratio (AR)span / chord; for any wing AR = b²/S
MACmean aerodynamic chord, the chord of an equivalent rectangular wing
Wash-in / wash-outwing incidence increasing / decreasing towards the tip
Fineness ratioratio of body length to maximum diameter
📖 Explained for complete beginners
A long, narrow wing (high AR) like a glider has low induced drag; a short, broad wing (low AR) like a fighter has the opposite.
PART 4/5 · 02

Tip vortices, upwash, downwash and airfoil contamination

  • High pressure under the wing leaks round the tip to the upper side and forms a vortex; vortices cause downwash behind the wing
  • Upwash is the upward flow just ahead of the wing when it is tilted to the stream
  • Ice, snow and frost on the wing roughen the surface, cut lift and raise drag; they must be removed before flight (EASA p. 2.9)
📖 Explained for complete beginners
Downwash is the source of induced drag: the wing "sees" a downward-tilted flow, so its lift vector tilts slightly backwards.
PART 5/5
05

Lift, drag and coefficients

  • L = ½ρV²·S·C_L and D = ½ρV²·S·C_D
  • The angle of attack (AOA) is the angle between the chord line and the relative wind
  • Drag is parasite drag plus induced drag
PART 5/5 · 02

Lift coefficient and the C_L versus angle curve

  • Lift is proportional to speed squared: an aircraft at 200 kt has four times the lift at 100 kt (EASA)
  • Thin symmetric airfoil theory: c_l = 2πα; at α = 1.5° it gives c_l = 0.164 and c_m,LE = −c_l/4 = -0.0411 (Anderson 4.5)
  • A NACA 2412 with c = 2 m, V = 50 m/s, ρ = 1.23 kg/m³ and L' = 1353 N/m gives c_l = 0.44, i.e. an angle of attack of about 2° (Anderson 4.2)
  • Beyond a critical angle the flow separates and C_L falls: the stall
📖 Explained for complete beginners
C_L is the normalisation: for one wing shape it tells the "effectiveness" regardless of whether the aircraft is big or small, fast or slow.
PART 5/5 · 02

Drag, the polar and the aerodynamic centre

  • Parasite drag includes form drag, skin friction and interference (EASA 2-7); it increases continuously with speed
  • Induced drag C_Di = C_L²/(π e AR) falls as speed rises and as AR grows
  • The polar plots C_L against C_D; the tangent from the origin gives the maximum L/D
  • The NACA 2412 aerodynamic centre is at x/c = 0.242, very near the quarter chord (Anderson 4.10)
📖 Explained for complete beginners
The two drags "trade places": slow flight has large induced drag, fast flight large parasite drag, so there is one speed with minimum total drag.
PART 5/5 · 02

Original figure: C_L, C_D and L/D against angle of attack

  • C_L rises almost linearly with angle of attack, peaks at C_Lmax and falls: the stall
  • L/D peaks at a much smaller angle of attack than C_Lmax
  • C_D rises sharply near the stall because of induced drag

📜 history

Bernoulli, Prandtl and understanding lift

Daniel Bernoulli published the pressure-velocity relation in Hydrodynamica (1738). Ludwig Prandtl introduced the boundary layer in 1904, explaining skin friction and flow separation. Lanchester, Kutta, Joukowski and Prandtl then built circulation theory and finite-wing theory; Anderson records these milestones in the Historical Note sections of chapters 3, 4 and 5.

🔎 case

Case: lift of a small wing (illustrative)

A hypothetical wing with S = 16 m², V = 50 m/s at ISA sea level (ρ = 1.225) and C_L = 0.65 gives L = ½ρV²SC_L = 15925 N, enough to lift about 1624 kg. Doubling the speed to 100 m/s at the same C_L gives 63700 N, four times as much, exactly as EASA says about 100 kt and 200 kt. The numbers are illustrative; the method is Bernoulli and the definition of C_L.

⚠️ watch out

Common pitfalls

(1) "The two air parcels must meet at the trailing edge at the same time" is a misconception; Bernoulli relates pressure and velocity and does not require equal transit times. (2) EASA says about three quarters of the lift comes from the upper-surface pressure drop; that is the EASA text's figure, not a physical constant. (3) Bernoulli (inviscid) does not apply once the flow has separated. (4) Chord is the along-stream dimension, not the span: aspect ratio is span / chord. (5) Anderson 4.1 and 4.2 use the NACA 2412 table: the same airfoil with ρ = 1.23 or 1.225 gives slightly different results, so read the stated assumptions before comparing answers.

1.5. Airfoil at α = 12°, c_n = 1.2, c_a = 0.03. Find c_l and c_d.

Anderson 6th, Problem 1.5

👁️ Show the solution

c_l = c_n cos α − c_a sin α = 1.168.

c_d = c_n sin α + c_a cos α = 0.279.

3.4 – 3.7. (3.4) Wind tunnel with area ratio 12 and a 10 cm Hg difference: V = ? (3.6) A Pitot reads 1.07×10⁵ N/m² at sea level: V = ? (3.7) Local V = 130 m/s: C_p = ?

Anderson 6th, Problems 3.4, 3.6, 3.7

👁️ Show the solution

What the problem says. Three problems on speed measurement: (3.4) a tunnel with area ratio 12 and a 10 cm Hg difference; (3.6) a Pitot tube reading 1.07×10⁵ N/m² at sea level; (3.7) a local velocity of 130 m/s on the wing of the aircraft in 3.6.

Idea. Bernoulli: p + ½ρV² is constant along a streamline. A known pressure difference gives the velocity; the pressure coefficient is C_p = 1 − (V/V∞)².

Steps.

  1. (3.4) Δp = ρ_Hg g h = 1.36×10⁴ × 9.81 × 0.1 = 13342 N/m².
  2. Combining Bernoulli and continuity (V₁ = V₂/12): Δp = ½ρV₂²(1 − 1/144) ⇒ V₂ = √[2Δp/(ρ(1 − 1/144))] = 147.8 m/s.
  3. (3.6) The Pitot tube reads p₀: p₀ − p = ½ρV² ⇒ V = √[2(1.07 − 1.01)×10⁵/1.23] = 98.8 m/s.
  4. (3.7) C_p = 1 − (130/98.8)² = -0.732: negative so the pressure is below the free stream, as on the suction region of a wing.

Check: Solution Manual: 147 m/s, 98.8 m/s and −0.73. In 3.4 the factor (1 − 1/144) changes the result by only about 0.35%.

4.2. NACA 2412, c = 2 m, V = 50 m/s, standard sea level, L' = 1353 N/m. Find the angle of attack.

Anderson 6th, Problem 4.2

👁️ Show the solution

q = 1537.5 N/m²; c_l = L'/(qc) = 0.44. NACA 2412 table (Problem 1.6): c_l = 0.44 at α = 2°.
