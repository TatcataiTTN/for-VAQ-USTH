02. Gas power cycles: Carnot, Otto, Diesel, Brayton and jet-propulsion cycles

From the Carnot cycle to Otto, Diesel, Brayton and the ideal jet-propulsion cycle: efficiency, net work, MEP, regeneration, intercooling and reheating.

Part 1 · Foundations

An idealised cycle keeps the main features of the real cycle and lets us compute the dominant parameters

Slide Ch.2 p.3–9

  • A power cycle produces work; a refrigeration cycle produces a cooling effect. A device running on a power cycle is an engine (p.4).
  • A gas cycle keeps the working fluid gaseous throughout; a vapor cycle has a liquid phase in part of it (p.5). In a closed cycle the fluid returns to its initial state and recirculates; in an open cycle it is renewed each cycle (p.6).
  • Internal combustion: the fuel burns inside the system boundary (automobile engines); external combustion: heat comes from an outside source (furnace, geothermal, reactor, sun; steam plants) (p.7).

📖 Explanation for beginners
Slide p.8 draws the real cycle (dashed, rounded corners) beside the ideal one (solid). We use the ideal cycle because it is close enough to compare design choices (more compression? higher temperature?) without modelling friction or burning rate.
Part 1 · Foundations

Thermal efficiency is net work over heat in, and the net work is the area inside the cycle

Thermal efficiency (slide p.10)
$$\eta_{th}=\frac{W_{net}}{Q_{in}}=1-\frac{Q_{out}}{Q_{in}}$$
  • $W_{net}$net work = area enclosed by the cycle on P–v (and q_net on T–s)
  • $Q_{in}$heat added to the cycle

Slide Ch.2 p.10 Added from the book: Cengel §9-1

  • Net work per kg: $w_{net}=\oint P\,dv=q_{in}-q_{out}$ (first law for a cycle, ΔU = 0).
  • The slide shows two diagrams: P–v (area = work) and T–s (area = heat). Both are used throughout the chapter.
📖 Explanation for beginners
A heat engine turns heat into work, so its efficiency is the work obtained per unit of heat paid for. The heat that does not become work (Q_out) is rejected, so η is always below 1.
Part 1 · Foundations

Carnot is the efficiency ceiling between two reservoirs but cannot be built, so we use Otto, Diesel and Brayton

Slide Ch.2 p.11–13 Added from the book: Cengel §9-2; Farokhi §4.3.1.6

  • Four reversible processes: 1→2 isothermal heat addition (TH), 2→3 isentropic expansion, 3→4 isothermal heat rejection (TL), 4→1 isentropic compression; executable in a closed system (piston–cylinder) or a steady-flow system (two turbines, two compressors) (pp.11–12).
Carnot efficiency (p.13): the highest between two reservoirs
$$\eta_{th,Carnot}=1-\frac{T_L}{T_H}\qquad(T\ \text{in K})$$
Example: source 1000 K, sink 300 K

$\eta=1-300/1000=0.70$: the theoretical ceiling 0.70 for any cycle between these two temperatures (the 0–0.7 axis of slide p.40). Practical ideal cycles are lower (Brayton at 8.2: 0.452).

Slide p.13: "it is not practical to build an engine that closely approximates Carnot; usable source and sink temperatures are limited". Farokhi §4.3.1.6 (p.194) shows it with ∮T ds: η = 1 − T₀/T_t4.

📖 Explanation for beginners
On T–s the Carnot cycle is a rectangle: two horizontal sides are isotherms, two vertical sides are isentropes. The formula depends only on the two absolute temperatures (kelvin); convert °C to K first.
Part 1 · Foundations

The air-standard assumptions turn every engine into an ideal-gas loop that receives and rejects heat

Slide Ch.2 p.14 Added from the book: Cengel §9-3

  • The working fluid is air, circulating in a closed loop and always an ideal gas.
  • All processes making up the cycle are internally reversible.
  • Combustion is replaced by heat addition from an external source (slide figure: real combustion chamber versus "Heating section").
  • The exhaust process is replaced by heat rejection that restores the initial state.

Part 2 · Methods

MEP converts the cycle's net work into one average pressure, so engines of equal size can be compared

Slide Ch.2 p.15–18 Added from the book: Cengel §9-4

Compression ratio and mean effective pressure (pp.15–16)
$$r=\frac{V_{max}}{V_{min}}=\frac{V_{BDC}}{V_{TDC}},\qquad MEP=\frac{W_{net}}{V_{max}-V_{min}}=\frac{w_{net}}{v_{max}-v_{min}}$$
  • $V_{TDC}$clearance volume at top dead centre
  • $V_{BDC}-V_{TDC}$displacement volume
  • $W_{net}=MEP\cdot V_d$MEP is the constant pressure giving the same net work over the stroke (p.17)
  • SI: a spark plug ignites the mixture; CI: air compressed above the self-ignition temperature, fuel injected (p.18).
  • Four strokes = 2 revolutions ⇒ power $\dot W=MEP\cdot V_d\cdot N/2$ (N: rev/s) Added from the book: Cengel 9–59
📖 Explanation for beginners
The figure on slide p.17 draws a rectangle of the same area as the cycle on P–V: its height is the MEP. A larger MEP gives more work per cycle for the same cylinder size. Example: a 2.4-L engine with MEP 900 kPa gives 2.16 kJ per cycle.
Part 2 · Methods

Otto efficiency depends only on the compression ratio and k: more compression, higher efficiency

Otto: q_in, q_out and efficiency (pp.22–24)
$$q_{in}=c_v(T_3-T_2),\;\; q_{out}=c_v(T_4-T_1),\;\;\boxed{\eta_{th,Otto}=1-\frac{1}{r^{\,k-1}}}\;,\;\; r=\frac{v_1}{v_2}$$
  • 1→2 isentropic; 2→3 constant-volume heat addition; 3→4 isentropic; 4→1 constant-volume rejection (p.20). Since T₁/T₂ = T₄/T₃ = r^(1−k), η depends only on r and k (pp.23–24).
  • Gasoline engines use r ≈ 7–10 (pink band p.25): η = 0.541 to 0.602 for k = 1.4.

Slide Ch.2 p.19–25 Added from the book: Cengel §9-5

📖 Explanation for beginners
The Otto cycle is the ideal cycle of the spark-ignition (gasoline) engine. Heat is added at constant volume (fast burn near top dead centre). The practical limit on r is knock of the gasoline–air mixture, so r stays around 7–10.
Part 2 · Methods

Diesel differs from Otto in isobaric heat addition; at equal compression ratio it is below Otto but runs at a much higher ratio

Diesel: cutoff ratio and efficiency (pp.29–31)
$$r_c=\frac{V_3}{V_2},\qquad q_{in}=c_p(T_3-T_2),\;\; q_{out}=c_v(T_4-T_1),\qquad\boxed{\eta_{th,Diesel}=1-\frac{1}{r^{k-1}}\left[\frac{r_c^{\,k}-1}{k\,(r_c-1)}\right]}$$
Same r = 16, k = 1.4ηOttoηDiesel (rc = 2)ηDiesel (rc = 3)
Efficiency0.6700.6140.569

Only process 2→3 differs from Otto (isobaric instead of isochoric, p.28). The bracket is always > 1 ⇒ ηOtto > ηDiesel at equal r (p.31); as rc → 1 Diesel → Otto. Real diesels still win because r = 12–24 (pp.27, 32); real large diesel engines reach about 35–40 % (p.32).

Slide Ch.2 p.26–32 Added from the book: Cengel §9-6

📖 Explanation for beginners
A diesel compresses air only so premature ignition is impossible; fuel burns as the injector sprays it into hot air. The cutoff ratio r_c = V₃/V₂ says how much of the stroke heat is added over: the larger r_c, the more heat but the lower the efficiency.
Part 2 · Methods

Brayton has the same efficiency structure as Otto, but compression and expansion run continuously in rotating machines, so it is described by a pressure ratio

Brayton: heats, efficiency, back work ratio (pp.37–39)
$$q_{in}=c_p(T_3-T_2),\;\; q_{out}=c_p(T_4-T_1),\;\;\boxed{\eta_{th,Brayton}=1-\frac{1}{r_p^{\,(k-1)/k}}},\;\; r_p=\frac{P_2}{P_1},\;\; r_{bw}=\frac{w_{comp}}{w_{turb}}$$
  • 1→2 isentropic compression (compressor); 2→3 isobaric heat addition (combustor); 3→4 isentropic expansion (turbine); 4→1 isobaric rejection (p.35). The real gas turbine runs on an open cycle, modelled as closed with two heat exchangers (pp.33–34).
  • Same form as Otto: exponent (k−1)/k and r_p = P₂/P₁. Use $T_2/T_1=r_p^{(k-1)/k}=T_3/T_4$ (p.38).

Slide Ch.2 p.33–39 Added from the book: Cengel §9-8

📖 Explanation for beginners
Remember the correspondence: Otto (isochoric, compression ratio r, exponent k−1) ↔ Brayton (isobaric, pressure ratio r_p, exponent (k−1)/k). For cold air the Brayton η does not depend on T₃, but T₃ fixes the net work. The compressor takes a large share of the turbine work: a back work ratio near 40 % in the Tutorials I example.
Part 2 · Methods

At fixed temperatures the Brayton net work peaks at one pressure ratio while efficiency keeps rising with r_p

r_p for maximum net work (p.40)
$$r_{p,w_{max}}=\left(\frac{T_{max}}{T_{min}}\right)^{k/[2(k-1)]}\;=\;(1000/300)^{1.75}=8.22$$

Net work rises then falls while efficiency keeps rising: maximum work and maximum efficiency are not at the same point. The left graph of p.40 (η versus r_p) lies above the k = 1.4 formula at large r_p: see the slide-error part.

Slide Ch.2 p.40

📖 Explanation for beginners
Why a peak? As r_p grows, T₂ after the compressor rises so the compressor work grows fast, while the turbine-exit temperature T₄ falls so the turbine work grows more slowly. At very large r_p, T₂ = T₃ and the net work is zero. Engineers choose r_p near the work maximum when they need a compact, light engine (aircraft) and higher when efficiency matters.

Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

Regeneration saves fuel at low r_p, but beyond the crossing the exhaust is cooler than the compressed air and regeneration is useless

Regenerator effectiveness and efficiency (pp.44–45)
$$\epsilon=\frac{q_{regen,act}}{q_{regen,max}}=\frac{h_5-h_2}{h_4-h_2}\;\cong\;\frac{T_5-T_2}{T_4-T_2},\qquad \eta_{th,regen}=1-\frac{T_1}{T_3}\,r_p^{(k-1)/k}$$

Crossing with the no-regeneration curve: $r_p=(T_3/T_1)^{k/[2(k-1)]}$ (the same rp as maximum work): beyond it T4 < T2 so there is nothing to regenerate. Slide p.44 typo: the ε denominator reads "regen,mã" = "regen,max".

Slide Ch.2 p.41–45 Added from the book: Cengel §9-9

📖 Explanation for beginners
Slide p.41 lists three ways to raise gas-turbine efficiency: a higher turbine inlet temperature (materials, blade cooling), higher component efficiencies, and added regeneration, intercooling and reheating (nearly doubling the efficiency of the first turbines). Regeneration (pp.42–45) uses the hot exhaust to preheat the air leaving the compressor before the combustor, so less fuel gives the same net work.
Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

Intercooling and reheating raise the net work but also the heat in, so they pay off only together with regeneration

  • Multistage compression with intercooling reduces compressor work; multistage expansion with reheating raises turbine work; equal stage pressure ratios are best (Pm = √(P1P3) for two stages) (pp.46–47).
  • Cengel Example 9–8 (rp = 8, 300 K, 1300 K): without regeneration rbw = 0.304, η = 0.358 (lower than the single stage 0.426); with an ideal regenerator η = 0.696.
  • Reheating is done by spraying extra fuel into the oxygen-rich exhaust (p.47). As the number of stages → ∞ the efficiency approaches the Carnot limit (p.48).

Slide Ch.2 p.46–48 Added from the book: Cengel §9-10

📖 Explanation for beginners
Steady-flow compression work is proportional to the specific volume of the gas, so cooling between stages (keeping v small) cuts the compressor work; reheating between expansion stages (keeping v large) raises the turbine work. But the heat added rises too (extra reheat heat), so η alone falls; the hotter exhaust and the cooler compressed air give regeneration more potential.

Part 5 · Aircraft application and summary

The turbojet is a truncated Brayton cycle: the turbine only drives the compressor and the rest of the energy becomes thrust in the nozzle

  • Unlike simple Brayton: the turbine expands only as far as needed to drive the compressor and auxiliaries; the gas still at high pressure expands further in the nozzle into kinetic energy. Net shaft work is zero (pp.49, 51).
  • Diffuser, compressor, turbine and nozzle are all isentropic; $T_2=T_1+\frac{V_1^2}{2c_p}$, $T_5=T_4-T_3+T_2$ (turbine work = compressor work).
Net thrust and the slide's "propulsive efficiency" (p.52)
$$F=\dot m\,(V_{exit}-V_{inlet}),\qquad \eta_P=\frac{\dot W_p}{\dot Q_{in}}=\frac{F\,V_{aircraft}}{\dot m\,c_p\,(T_4-T_3)}$$

The slide/Cengel quantity η_P (thrust power over heat input) corresponds to the overall efficiency η_o of Farokhi and El-Sayed; their propulsive efficiency is 2V/(V + V_e) (m01).

Slide Ch.2 p.49–52 Added from the book: Cengel §9-11; Farokhi §4.3.1.7; El-Sayed §4.2

📖 Explanation for beginners
In an aircraft the net shaft work is zero but the thrust is not: the momentum of the gas leaving the nozzle exceeds that of the gas entering. This cycle links chapter 2 to chapter 1: the temperatures and pressures found here give the jet speed used in the thrust formula of m01.

📖 Explanation for beginners
Fixed order of calculation: (1) the diffuser raises T and P from flight kinetic energy; (2) the compressor raises them more; (3) the burner brings T to the turbine limit; (4) the turbine lowers T by exactly the compressor rise (T₅ = T₄ − T₃ + T₂); (5) the nozzle expands to ambient pressure, producing V₆. From V₆ follows the thrust F = ṁ(V₆ − V).

3. Theory and history context

The slides state: Nikolaus Otto built a successful four-stroke engine in 1876 in Germany (p.20); Rudolph Diesel proposed the cycle in the 1890s (p.26); George Brayton proposed the cycle for a reciprocating oil-burning engine (p.33), now used for gas turbines only. The dates 1876 and 1890s are taken verbatim from the slides, NOT checked against a third source. Relation between cycles: Otto and Diesel are two ends of the same internal-combustion cycle (isochoric or isobaric heat addition); Cengel §9-6 also mentions the dual cycle that contains both. Brayton is Otto's sibling: isochoric Otto with compression ratio r, isobaric Brayton with pressure ratio r_p, with the same 1 − 1/x^k form. The ideal jet cycle is Brayton with the turbine expanding only part way, the rest becoming kinetic energy (p.49).

Two ways to compare Otto and Diesel (Cengel §9-6): at the same compression ratio Otto is more efficient (bracket > 1); at the same peak pressure Diesel is more efficient because it can compress to a higher r without exceeding P_max. The slides give only the first case (p.31); the second is a book supplement with NO dedicated question yet.

4. Aircraft application

From the slides: the gas turbine (p.3) is the core of the turbojet, turbofan and turboprop (pp.51–54); the ramjet has no rotating machines (p.55). From El-Sayed Example 4.2 (the J85-GT-13A of the Fiat G91Y, 12.12 kN per engine): the ideal cycle gives f = 0.0198, V_j = 1,036 m/s, ṁ_a = 16.2 kg/s per engine, η_o = 0.273. The 1400 K turbine inlet and r_p = 12 are the example's numbers, not published J85 data. From Cengel Example 9–9: an aircraft at 850 ft/s flying at 5 psia with r_p = 10 gives V_6 = 3,286 ft/s. Any other number about real engines is illustrative.

5. Exam pitfalls

  • Always convert °C to K (and °F to R) before using T₂ = T₁r^(k−1) or the Carnot η; 17 °C = 290 K (or 290.15), not 17.
  • Otto uses the exponent k − 1 with the compression ratio r; Brayton uses (k − 1)/k with the pressure ratio r_p. Mixing the two exponents is the most frequent error.
  • Diesel: isobaric heat addition ⇒ c_p; isochoric rejection ⇒ c_v. If the statement gives T_max, r_c is unknown: r_c = T₃/T₂.
  • MEP divides by (v₁ − v₂) = v₁(1 − 1/r), not v₁. Four-stroke: power = W_cycle · N/2.
  • If the statement says "variable specific heats" ⇒ use Table A-17 (P_r for Brayton compression/expansion, v_r for Otto/Diesel); 1 − 1/r^(k−1) is only approximate.
  • Real Brayton: w_comp = w_s/η_c (divide), w_turb = η_t·w_s (multiply). A poor compressor raises T₂, but do not use T₂s in q_in.
  • Regeneration helps only when T₄ > T₂ (r_p below (T₃/T₁)^(k/[2(k−1)])). Intercooling + reheating alone lower the efficiency.
  • The "propulsive efficiency" of the slide/Cengel (thrust power/heat in) ≠ the propulsive efficiency 2V/(V + V_e) of Farokhi/El-Sayed. Read the definition the question uses.
  • Printed book answers may rest on a hidden assumption (Cengel 9–96, 9–119: Table A-17 although the statement is silent) or contain errors (9–59: AF 25 instead of 24; El-Sayed 4.2: two arithmetic slips).
