MODULE 01 · Atmosphere

Physics of the Atmosphere and the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA)

An aircraft operates in air, so every aerodynamic force depends on three properties of that air: pressure, density and temperature. This module goes from pressure and its units, through density and humidity, to the change with altitude and the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA), then applies it to real problems: air density on a Boeing 727 wing, a World War I Zeppelin and a hot high-altitude airport.

EASA Module 08, Sub-module 01 (pp. 1.1–1.8) · Anderson ch.1 sec. 1.4 and Appendix D · Etkin & Reid Appendix D

PART 1/5
01

Air is a fluid

  • Air is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, and is a fluid because it can flow and take the shape of its container
  • Unlike water, air is compressible
  • Aerodynamics studies bodies moving through air and the forces produced
PART 1/5 · 01

What aerodynamics is and why a technician needs it

  • "aero" means air and "dyne" means force: aerodynamics studies objects moving through air and the forces that produce or change that motion
  • Knowing why surfaces must be aerodynamically smooth and why primary and secondary controls exist is essential to maintain modern aircraft
  • Aerodynamically, an aircraft is an object travelling through space and affected by changes in atmospheric conditions
📖 Explained for complete beginners
Imagine putting your hand out of a moving car window: it feels a force although it touches nothing solid. Air moving relative to a body produces force, and that is what this whole course describes.
PART 1/5 · 01

Three air properties: p, ρ, T

  • Pressure p (N/m² or Pa): force per unit area
  • Density ρ (kg/m³): mass per unit volume
  • Temperature T (kelvin): a measure of the thermal motion of the molecules
  • At near-standard conditions the three are linked by the perfect-gas equation of state p = ρRT with R = 287 J/(kg·K) (Anderson 1.1)
📖 Explained for complete beginners
Knowing two of the three gives the third. Remember to convert temperature to kelvin: K = °C + 273.15.
PART 2/5
02

Atmospheric pressure and its units

  • Atmospheric pressure is the weight of the air column above divided by the area
  • Standard sea level: 1 atm = 14.7 psi = 29.92 inHg = 1013.2 mb (hPa) = 760 mmHg
  • Pressure falls quickly with altitude
PART 2/5 · 01

P = F/A and the barometer

  • Force F = area A × pressure P, so P = F/A
  • A one-square-inch column of air from sea level to the top of the atmosphere weighs about 14.7 lb, so sea-level pressure is 14.7 psi
  • A barometer uses a mercury column and reads pressure in inches of mercury (inHg)
  • Meteorology now uses the hectopascal: 1 hPa = 100 Pa
📖 Explained for complete beginners
Pressure is not a separate force: it is force spread over an area, just as the same weight presses deeper through a stiletto heel than through a flat sole.

PART 2/5 · 01

Pressure decreases with altitude

  • The air column above is shorter and lighter, so pressure drops
  • At 40 000 ft pressure is only 2.72 psi (EASA Figure 1-4 gives 2.72 psi), about one sixth of the sea-level value
  • At 50 000 ft pressure is almost one tenth of sea level (EASA)
  • As an aircraft climbs, pressure, oxygen and temperature all drop, affecting lift and engine power
📖 Explained for complete beginners
The 2.72 psi at 40 000 ft is checked with the ISA formula below and agrees to two decimals.
PART 3/5
03

Density and humidity

  • Density is proportional to pressure and inversely proportional to temperature
  • Humid air is lighter than dry air
  • Low density reduces lift and thrust and lengthens the take-off run
PART 3/5 · 01

Two rules for density

  • Density varies directly with pressure
  • Density varies inversely with temperature
  • So high-altitude air is thinner than low-altitude air, and a hot air mass is thinner than a cold one
  • An aircraft can fly faster at high altitude because there are fewer air particles per unit volume, so less resistance
📖 Explained for complete beginners
Both rules follow from p = ρRT: at constant T density rises with p; at constant p density falls as T rises.
PART 3/5 · 01

Humidity makes air lighter

  • Absolute humidity: mass of water vapour per unit volume of air
  • Relative humidity: the percentage ratio of the water vapour present to the amount at saturation at the same temperature and pressure
  • Water vapour weighs about 5/8 as much as the same volume of dry air, so humid air has lower density
  • On damp days an aircraft needs a longer take-off runway than on dry days
📖 Explained for complete beginners
Many people think humid air is "heavy". In fact a water molecule (mass 18) replaces a nitrogen or oxygen molecule (28 and 32), so the mixture gets lighter.
PART 4/5
04

Temperature and the atmospheric layers

  • Troposphere: temperature falls about 2 °C per 1 000 ft
  • The tropopause is about −57 °C
  • Most civil aviation takes place in the troposphere
PART 4/5 · 01

Troposphere and tropopause

  • On average the troposphere reaches about 38 000 ft; over the poles only 25 000–30 000 ft, at the equator about 60 000 ft
  • Temperature falls almost steadily, about −2 °C or −3.5 °F per 1 000 ft
  • The upper boundary is the tropopause, a zone of nearly constant −57 °C (−69 °F)
📖 Explained for complete beginners
−2 °C per 1 000 ft is about −6.5 °C per km, exactly the 0.0065 K/m gradient used in ISA below.
PART 4/5 · 01

The layers above

  • Stratosphere: temperature rises with altitude to near 0 °C; it holds the ozone layer that blocks ultraviolet rays
  • Mesosphere: temperature falls again
  • The thermosphere lies above
  • Some civil and many military flights take place in the stratosphere
📖 Explained for complete beginners
Temperature does not fall forever with height: in the stratosphere ozone absorbs ultraviolet and the air warms. Note that ISA holds temperature constant from 11 to 20 km, so the ISA table does not show this warming.
PART 5/5
05

The International Standard Atmosphere (ISA)

  • The real atmosphere always changes, so a standard model is needed for comparison and calibration
  • ISA at sea level: 15 °C, 1013.25 hPa, density 1.225 kg/m³
  • Published by ICAO, ISO and governments

PART 5/5 · 01

Application: density altitude

  • ISA underlies the altimeter, aircraft performance figures and comparisons between manufacturers
  • Illustrative example: an airport at 5 000 ft and 30 °C (about 25 °C above ISA). The real density 0.969 kg/m³ is only 79% of standard sea level, equivalent to a density altitude of about 7801 ft
  • That is why take-off performance suffers on hot days at high airports
📖 Explained for complete beginners
Density altitude is the altitude in ISA that has the same density as the actual air. It tells how high the aircraft effectively "feels" it is.

📜 history

From Torricelli's tube to ISA

In 1643 Evangelista Torricelli built the first mercury barometer and showed that air has weight. In 1648 Florin Périer, at Blaise Pascal's request, carried a barometer up the Puy de Dôme and saw the mercury column fall at height, confirming that pressure drops with altitude. Later ICAO, ISO and governments agreed one standard atmosphere so that every altimeter and performance table share a common baseline (EASA, p. 1.5).

🔎 case

Case: a Zeppelin and a Boeing 727 wing

Anderson 1.1: at a point on a Boeing 727 wing, p = 1.9×10⁴ N/m² and T = 203 K, so ρ = p/RT = 0.326 kg/m³, about a third of sea-level density. Anderson 1.12: a 15 000 m³ Zeppelin flying at 1 000 m (ISA ρ = 1.1116 kg/m³) is supported by Archimedes buoyancy of 1.634×10⁵ N plus aerodynamic lift of 3850 N, so the total weight is 1.673×10⁵ N. Buoyancy carries almost all the weight; aerodynamic lift is only 2.3%.

⚠️ watch out

Common pitfalls

(1) Humid air is lighter, not heavier, than dry air. (2) Pressure does not fall linearly with altitude but roughly exponentially, so at 40 000 ft about 1/6 remains, not 1/3. (3) The −2 °C per 1 000 ft gradient holds only in the troposphere; above the tropopause ISA temperature is constant. (4) Anderson 1.12 includes Archimedes buoyancy: forgetting it makes the weight wrong by a large factor. (5) The Solution Manual (4th ed., checked on the page image, p. 12) prints L = 3487 N on the lift line, but 500 × 153.9 × 0.05 = 3847 N and the very next line uses 3847 N to reach W = 1.67×10⁵ N: the final answer is right and only the middle line has a typo.

✍️ Worked problems

Three Anderson end-of-chapter-1 problems solved step by step. Data are from the 6th edition; solutions are recomputed here and cross-checked against the Solution Manual (4th edition, handwritten).

1.1. (a) p = 1.9×10⁴ N/m², T = 203 K: find ρ. (b) p = 1058 lb/ft², ρ = 1.23×10⁻³ slug/ft³: find T.

Anderson 6th, Problem 1.1

👁️ Show the solution

(a) ρ = p/(RT) = 1.9×10⁴/(287×203) = 0.326 kg/m³.

(b) T = p/(ρR) = 1058/(1.23×10⁻³×1716) = 501 °R.

Matches the Solution Manual.

1.11. U-tube mercury manometer: 20 cm difference, high column on the model side, p_atm = 1.01×10⁵ N/m². Find the pressure on the model wing.

Anderson 6th, Problem 1.11

👁️ Show the solution

The high column is on the low-pressure side: p = p_atm − ρ_Hg g h = 1.01×10⁵ − (1.36×10⁴)(9.8)(0.2) = 74344 N/m² (7.43×10⁴).

1.12. Zeppelin: volume 15 000 m³, d_max = 14 m, V = 30 m/s at a standard 1 000 m, C_L = 0.05. Find the total weight.

Anderson 6th, Problem 1.12

👁️ Show the solution

Equilibrium: W = B + L.

B = ρVg = 1.1117×15 000×9.8 = 163411 N. q = ½ρV² = 500.2 Pa. S = π·14²/4 = 153.9 m². L = qSC_L = 3850 N.

W = 167262 N (≈ 1.673×10⁵ N).

The Solution Manual misprints L = 3487 N on the middle line (rounding q to 500 Pa gives 3847 N, unrounded gives 3850 N); its final 1.67×10⁵ N is right.
