05. Compressible flow

From stagnation enthalpy and isentropic relations in Ma to the choked converging nozzle, ṁ_max and A/A*; the slide examples with CO₂, air and nitrogen, the √1000 unit trap and the printing errors of slides and books.

PART 1/5
01

Foundations: stagnation enthalpy, temperature and pressure

  • Slides Ch.5 pp.3–9
  • h₀ = h + V²/2 is constant in adiabatic flow without work
  • T₀ = T + V²/2c_p; P₀/P = (T₀/T)^(k/(k−1))
Part 1 · Foundations

In adiabatic flow without work the stagnation enthalpy h₀ = h + V²/2 stays constant, and stopping the flow turns kinetic energy into enthalpy

Slide Ch.5 p.3–6

  • The specific enthalpy is h = u + Pv (p.3). When the kinetic and potential energies of the fluid are negligible h represents the total energy of the fluid; in high-speed flows such as in jet engines the potential energy is still negligible but the kinetic energy is not (p.3).
  • The stagnation (total) enthalpy combines enthalpy and kinetic energy: $h_0=h+\dfrac{V^2}{2}$ [kJ/kg] (1; p.4). h is the static enthalpy, h₀ the stagnation enthalpy.
  • Steady adiabatic flow through a duct (nozzle, diffuser…) with no shaft work and constant potential energy: $h_1+\dfrac{V_1^2}{2}=h_2+\dfrac{V_2^2}{2}$, i.e. $h_{01}=h_{02}$: the stagnation enthalpy stays constant (p.5).
  • If the flow is brought to a complete stop (V₂ = 0): $h_{02}=h_2=h_1+V_1^2/2$. The kinetic energy is converted to enthalpy (internal energy + flow energy), so temperature and pressure rise; the properties at the stagnation state are the stagnation properties (p.6).
📖 Explanation for beginners
Think of a moving car braking hard: the kinetic energy does not vanish, it becomes heat. A gas is the same: when it is stopped it heats up and is compressed. The "stagnation enthalpy" is the total (thermal energy + kinetic energy) the gas carries; with no heat or work in or out the total stays fixed along the nozzle and only shifts between h and V²/2.
Part 1 · Foundations

The stagnation temperature T₀ = T + V²/(2c_p) matters only from a few hundred m/s: 100 m/s gives 5 K, 1000 m/s nearly 500 K

Slide Ch.5 p.7

Stagnation temperature (ideal gas, constant c_p; p.7)
$$c_pT_0=c_pT+\frac{V^2}{2}\ \Rightarrow\ T_0=T+\frac{V^2}{2c_p}$$
  • $T_0$stagnation (total) temperature: the temperature the gas reaches when brought to rest adiabatically
  • $V^2/2c_p$dynamic temperature

A stationary probe in a gas flow reads T₀, not T. Air at 300 K, c_p = 1.005 kJ/(kg·K) (p.14; answers computed here because the slide prints none):

📖 Explanation for beginners
V² is in the formula, so the dynamic temperature grows very fast with speed: ten times the speed gives a hundred times the temperature rise. At road speed (30 m/s) the difference is under half a degree and nobody notices; at jet speeds a probe or a wing leading edge heats by tens of degrees.
Part 1 · Foundations

The stagnation pressure P₀ = P(T₀/T)^(k/(k−1)) and the compressor work w = c_p(T₀₂ − T₀₁) use stagnation quantities throughout

Slide Ch.5 p.8

Stagnation pressure and density (p.8): isentropic stop
$$\frac{P_0}{P}=\left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^{k/(k-1)}\ (2)\qquad \frac{\rho_0}{\rho}=\left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^{1/(k-1)}\ (3)$$
  • $k$specific-heat ratio c_p/c_v (air 1.4)
  • $Pv^k=P_0v_0^k$isentropic relation used to derive (3)
  • The stagnation pressure P₀ is the pressure the gas reaches when brought to rest isentropically (p.8).
  • One-stream energy balance with stagnation enthalpy (p.9): $q_{in}+w_{in}+(h_{01}+gz_1)=q_{out}+w_{out}+(h_{02}+gz_2)$ (4); ideal gas with constant c_p: $(q_{in}-q_{out})+(w_{in}-w_{out})=c_p(T_{02}-T_{01})+g(z_2-z_1)$ (5). It applies to compressors, turbines and combustors when T₀ is known.
📖 Explanation for beginners
A compressor or turbine receives a moving gas, so the proper "inlet" is the stagnation state, not the static one. Just convert static T, P into T₀, P₀ (V appears in the formula) and then apply the familiar isentropic formulas of Chapter 2 to T₀, P₀.

Methods: Hugoniot, isentropic relations in Ma, critical properties, choked flow, A/A*

  • Slides Ch.5 pp.17, 25–49

Part 2 · Methods

The Hugoniot relation dA/A = −(dV/V)(1 − Ma²) fixes the shape: subsonic flow accelerates as the duct narrows, supersonic flow as it widens

Slide Ch.5 p.17 Slide Ch.5 p.25–31

  • One-dimensional flow: quantities vary only along the flow (p.17). Mass: $\dot m=\rho AV=\text{const}\Rightarrow \dfrac{d\rho}{\rho}+\dfrac{dA}{A}+\dfrac{dV}{V}=0$ (*); isentropic energy: $\dfrac{dP}{\rho}+V\,dV=0$ (**) (p.25–26).
  • Combining (*), (**) with $(\partial\rho/\partial P)_s=1/c^2$ gives $\dfrac{dA}{A}=\dfrac{dP}{\rho V^2}(1-\mathrm{Ma}^2)$ (6, 7; p.27–28), and with $\rho V=-dP/dV$ the Hugoniot relation $\dfrac{dA}{A}=-\dfrac{dV}{V}(1-\mathrm{Ma}^2)$ (p.30).

📖 Explanation for beginners
For water or slow air, narrowing the duct speeds the flow up: everyday intuition. Once the flow is supersonic the gas expands faster than the area widens, so to accelerate it further the duct must WIDEN. At Ma = 1 the two effects balance: that is the throat, the smallest area. So going from subsonic to supersonic needs a converging then diverging duct.

Part 2 · Methods

The maximum mass flow of a nozzle depends only on A*, P₀, T₀ and the gas; one A/A* has two Ma solutions

Slide Ch.5 p.44–49

Mass flow, maximum and area ratio (p.44–49)
$$\dot m=\frac{A\,\mathrm{Ma}\,P_0\sqrt{k/(RT_0)}}{\left[1+(k-1)\mathrm{Ma}^2/2\right]^{\frac{k+1}{2(k-1)}}}\ (15)\quad \dot m_{max}=A^*P_0\sqrt{\frac{k}{RT_0}}\left(\frac{2}{k+1}\right)^{\frac{k+1}{2(k-1)}}\ (16)\quad \frac{A}{A^*}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Ma}}\left[\frac{2}{k+1}\left(1+\frac{k-1}{2}\mathrm{Ma}^2\right)\right]^{\frac{k+1}{2(k-1)}}\ (17)$$
Redrawn: one A/A* > 1 corresponds to two Ma. Orange points: the slide examples. (click the image for full size)
  • ṁ is maximum when Ma = 1, i.e. at the smallest section; ṁ_max ∝ A*P₀/√T₀ (p.45–46, 48): raising P₀ or lowering T₀ increases ṁ.
  • The unit trap in (16): use P₀ [Pa], R [J/(kg·K)], A [m²]. Plugging kPa and kJ/(kg·K) in directly is wrong by a factor √1000 = 31.6 (slide p.55: 0.2246 instead of 7.103 kg/s).
📖 Explanation for beginners
Equation (15) shows that ṁ depends on Ma through a function with a maximum: at small Ma the gas is slow, at large Ma it is too thin, and the maximum is exactly at Ma = 1. So when the throat reaches Ma = 1 the nozzle is "choked": lowering the pressure behind it does not push more gas through. And (17) ties area to Ma; since A/A* has a minimum of 1 at Ma = 1, each A/A* > 1 has one subsonic and one supersonic root.
Part 2 · Methods

For a converging nozzle, lowering the back pressure to P* raises the flow to its maximum; lowering it further changes neither the flow nor P_e = P*

Slide Ch.5 p.39–43 Slide Ch.5 p.47–48

Redrawn from the formulas; the two red dots are the two cases of the slide example. (click the image for full size)
  • The stagnation pressure and temperature at every section equal those of the reservoir (V_r = 0, isentropic; p.41).
  • P_b = P₀: no flow (p.42). P* < P_b < P₀: pressure falls along the nozzle, P_e = P_b, not choked. P_b = P*: maximum ṁ, choked flow (p.43).
  • P_b < P*: nothing changes any more inside the nozzle; P_e = P* (p.43, 47). The rest of the expansion happens outside.
📖 Explanation for beginners
Picture a blowpipe: the harder you suck (the lower the back pressure) the more gas passes, but once the gas speed at the mouth reaches the speed of sound the news "the outside pressure is even lower" cannot travel back into the pipe because news travels at the speed of sound. So the inside does not learn about the lower pressure and the flow stays put.

📖 Explanation for beginners

Every step is a Chapter 2 isentropic formula, just applied to the stagnation state.

Part 3 · Worked examples

P_b = 0.7 MPa is not choked and gives 6.77 kg/s; P_b = 0.4 MPa is choked and gives at most 7.10 kg/s at P_t = 552 kPa

Slide Ch.5 p.50–55 Added from the book: Cengel Ex 17–5

  • Statement (p.50): air at 1 MPa, 600 °C, 150 m/s enters a converging nozzle, A_t = 50 cm². Stagnation (p.51): T₀ = 873 + 150²/2010 = 884.2 K; P₀ = 1 × (884.2/873)^3.5 = 1.046 MPa. P*/P₀ = 0.5283 (p.52).
  • (a) P_b = 0.7 MPa: P_b/P₀ = 0.670 > 0.5283 ⇒ not choked, P_t = P_b. A-32 (p.53): Ma_t = 0.778; T_t = 0.892 × 884 = 788.5 K; ρ_t = 700/(0.287 × 788.5) = 3.093 kg/m³; V_t = 437.9 m/s; ṁ = 3.093 × 50×10⁻⁴ × 437.9 = 6.77 kg/s (exact formula: Ma_t = 0.7794, ṁ = 6.785 kg/s).
  • (b) P_b = 0.4 MPa: P_b/P₀ = 0.383 < 0.5283 ⇒ CHOKED, Ma_t = 1, P_t = P* = 552 kPa (not 400). ṁ_max = A*P₀√(k/(RT₀))(2/(k+1))³ = 50×10⁻⁴ × 1.045×10⁶ Pa × √(1.4/(287 × 884)) × 0.5787 = 7.103 kg/s (slide 7.10).
The unit trap on slide p.55

The slide writes (50×10⁻⁴ m²)(1045 kPa)√(1.4/((0.287 kJ/kg·K)(884 K)))(2/2.4)^(2.4/0.8) with no unit conversion. Taken literally it gives 0.2246 kg/s; the slide result 7.10 is right because P₀ must be converted to Pa (×10³) and R to J/(kg·K) (×10³), i.e. an extra √1000 = 31.62: 0.2246 × 31.62 = 7.10. Recorded in the source-errors list.

📖 Explanation for beginners
The first question is always: is P_b larger than P*? If yes the flow is not choked: the throat pressure equals P_b, look up Ma from P_t/P₀ then compute ρ, V, ṁ. If not the flow is choked: the throat is at Ma = 1, its pressure is P*, and ṁ comes from formula (16). The flow in (b) is only about 5 % above (a) although the back pressure fell by almost half, because it is already near its maximum.

Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

The five most common errors are units (kPa/Pa, kJ/J), the Ma branch, P_t when choked, K/°C and the squared velocity

Slide Ch.5 p.55 Slide Ch.5 p.20 Slide Ch.5 p.58–59

  • Units in (16): P₀ must be in Pa and R in J/(kg·K) (or both in kPa and kJ/(kg·K) with √(k/(RT₀)) multiplied by √1000). Every unit-trap question of the module has this form.
  • V² in units: V²/(2c_p) with V [m/s] gives m²/s²; divide by 1000 to get kJ/kg. Slide p.20 misprints m²/s³.
  • Two Ma roots of A/A*: pick the branch from the context (converging duct, subsonic start: subsonic root; past the throat: supersonic).
  • P_t when choked is P*, not P_b. Compute ρ_t, V_t with P* (or use (16) directly).
  • Stagnation temperatures in K, never °C, in every ratio T₀/T.

Unit-trap questions: the question bank has the topic "Unit traps (kPa/Pa, kJ/kg·K)".

📖 Explanation for beginners
Most points lost in this chapter come not from misunderstanding the theory but from unit conversions. The safe way: write the unit on every line, convert everything to SI (Pa, J/(kg·K), m², K) before substituting into (16), and compare the result with a plausible value (a 50 cm² nozzle at 1 MPa passes a few kg/s, not 0.2 or 200).

Part 5 · Aircraft application and summary

Five fixed steps solve every nozzle problem: reduce to the stagnation state, compare P_b with P*, find Ma, derive the quantities, then check units

Slide Ch.5 p.10–59

  1. Step 1: reduce to the stagnation state. Turn every static datum (T, P, V or Ma) into T₀ = T + V²/2c_p (K) and P₀ = P(T₀/T)^(k/(k−1)); for a compressor/turbine get T₀₂ from the stagnation pressure ratio.
  2. Step 2: compute P*. P* = P₀ × (2/(k+1))^(k/(k−1)) (air: 0.5283). Compare P_b with P*: P_b ≥ P* means not choked (P_e = P_b), P_b < P* means choked (P_e = P*).
  3. Step 3: find Ma at the section you need. From P/P₀ invert (10); from A/A* use (17) and choose the branch from the duct shape and the inlet Ma.
  4. Step 4: derive T, ρ, V and ṁ. T = T₀·T/T₀; ρ = P/(RT); V = Ma√(kRT); ṁ = ρVA, or (16) for choked flow (convert to Pa and J/(kg·K)).
  5. Step 5: check. Units on every line, whether Ma and the trends agree with the Hugoniot table, ṁ ≤ ṁ_max, and A-32 only at printed rows.
📖 Explanation for beginners
This sequence is the common outline of the slide examples p.10–13, 50–55 and 56–59: three different statements with the same mould. Facing an unfamiliar problem, ask in turn: what is the stagnation state, is the nozzle choked, what is Ma at the section I need.

3. Theory context

All formulas of the chapter follow from three laws: mass conservation (ρAV constant), adiabatic energy without work (h + V²/2 constant) and the isentropy of steady frictionless flow. For an ideal gas with constant c_p, differentiating these gives the Hugoniot relation and integrating gives the ratios in Ma. The Mach number appears naturally because the speed of sound c² = (∂P/∂ρ)_s = kRT is the propagation speed of small disturbances; when V exceeds c a disturbance cannot travel upstream, which is why the flow chokes. The model neglects friction and heat transfer (Fanno, Rayleigh) and shock waves (Cengel §17-5, 17-6, 17-7): NOT DONE.

5. Exam pitfalls

  • ṁ_max = A*P₀√(k/RT₀)(2/(k+1))^((k+1)/(2(k−1))): P₀ in Pa, R in J/(kg·K), A in m²; plugging kPa and kJ directly is wrong by √1000.
  • A converging nozzle reaches Ma = 1 only at the exit; P_e = max(P_b, P*) (in pressure, not in the temperature ratio).
  • Convert °C to K before forming T₀/T; V²/(2c_p) needs V [m/s] and c_p [kJ/kg·K] with a ÷ 1000.
  • One A/A* has two Ma; choose the branch from the duct shape and the inlet Ma.
  • A book's printed answer may use variable c_p (17–11) or k at the stagnation temperature (17–40); do not interpolate A-32 between sparse rows (errors up to +120 %).
  • Cengel's FE items ask for the nearest option (17–153: 274 m/s ↔ 280 m/s).
