MODULE 04 · Stability

Flight stability and dynamics: aircraft axes, static and dynamic stability, oscillation modes

An aircraft must return to equilibrium after a disturbance and must respond properly to control inputs. This module starts with the three axes and three control surfaces, separates static from dynamic stability, then covers longitudinal stability (neutral point, static margin), lateral and directional stability, and ends with the real oscillation modes of the Boeing 747: phugoid and short period, with eigenvalues taken from the system matrix in Etkin and recomputed in Python. Two live demos let you drag the centre of gravity and simulate the response.

EASA Module 08, Sub-module 04 (pp. 4.1–4.12) · Etkin & Reid ch. 1, 2, 3, 6 · Anderson ch. 4 sec. 4.9

PART 1/5
01

Three axes and three control surfaces

  • The longitudinal axis runs nose to tail, the lateral axis wingtip to wingtip and the vertical axis top to bottom
  • Roll, pitch and yaw are controlled by the ailerons, elevator and rudder
  • Stability, manoeuvrability and controllability are three different ideas

📖 Explained for complete beginners
The names are confusing: longitudinal stability is about the LATERAL axis, because pitching rotates the aircraft about the lateral axis.
PART 1/5 · 04

Stability, manoeuvrability and controllability

  • Stability: the characteristic that makes an aircraft fly straight and level hands off
  • Manoeuvrability: the characteristic that lets the aircraft be directed along a desired path and withstand the stresses imposed
  • Controllability: the quality of the aircraft's response to the pilot's control application
  • Too much stability makes the aircraft hard to manoeuvre: the two trade off against each other
📖 Explained for complete beginners
Fighters are designed near neutral or even unstable for agility, with a flight-control computer providing the stability.
PART 2/5
02

Static and dynamic stability

  • Static stability: the initial tendency after a disturbance (return, stay, or move away from equilibrium)
  • Dynamic stability: the response over time, with amplitude decreasing, constant or growing
  • Positive static stability does not guarantee positive dynamic stability

PART 3/5
03

Longitudinal stability: centre of gravity, tail and the neutral point

  • Static longitudinal stability depends on the wing location relative to the CG, the tail location relative to the CG and the tail area
  • The centre of lift of a cambered wing moves forward as AOA grows: the wing alone is unstable
  • The condition for stability: the CG lies ahead of the neutral point (positive static margin)
PART 3/5 · 04

Three factors and the CG-CL-T lever

  • The wing lifts at the centre of lift CL aft of the CG, so the aircraft tends to be nose-heavy
  • The horizontal tail at a small negative angle produces a downward force at T to balance the moment
  • Lower speed reduces downwash and the download on the tail, the nose drops and speed rises again: the root of the phugoid (EASA Figure 4-5)
  • Reducing power lets the nose pitch down; a thrust line above or below the CG also affects longitudinal stability (EASA Figures 4-6 to 4-8)
📖 Explained for complete beginners
The horizontal tail acts like the fletching of an arrow: it pulls the rear into the airstream so the nose keeps pointing the way it flies.
PART 3/5 · 04

Neutral point and static margin (Etkin 2.3)

  • The neutral point h_n is the CG position for which C_mα = 0: the boundary between positive and negative pitch stiffness
  • C_mα = C_Lα (h − h_n) and static margin K_n = h_n − h; stable when h < h_n, i.e. K_n > 0 (CG ahead of the NP)
  • A typical requirement: the NP lies behind the most aft CG by about 5% of the mean aerodynamic chord (Etkin 1.5)
  • Illustrative example: h = 0.25, h_n = 0.35, C_Lα = 4.5 per rad gives K_n = 0.10 and C_mα = -0.45 per rad
📖 Explained for complete beginners
Move the CG aft to the neutral point and C_mα goes to zero: the aircraft loses pitch stiffness. That is why the aft CG limit is a safety limit for loading and maintenance.

PART 4/5
04

Lateral and directional stability

  • Dihedral raises lateral stability: an increase in wing dihedral increases lateral stability (EASA Q4-5)
  • Sweepback, the keel effect and weight distribution also contribute
  • The fin and rear fuselage give directional stability; Dutch roll is a coupled yaw-roll oscillation
PART 4/5 · 04

Lateral stability

  • When a wing drops the aircraft slips towards the low wing; with dihedral the low wing meets the air at a larger effective angle of attack, so its lift grows and levels the aircraft
  • Sweepback acts like dihedral
  • Keel effect: the fuselage and fin above or below the CG create a rolling moment when sideslipping
  • Weight low relative to the wing (the pendulum effect) also helps, but only slightly
📖 Explained for complete beginners
Etkin sec. 3.11 calls this roll stiffness, the derivative C_lβ.
PART 4/5 · 04

Directional stability and Dutch roll

  • The vertical fin acts like a weather vane: when the aircraft sideslips the fin produces a yawing moment that turns the nose into the wind (weathercock stability, Etkin 3.9)
  • Dutch roll is a coupled yawing and rolling oscillation, named after the motion of an ice skater (Etkin Exercise 6.11)
  • Directional stability that is strong relative to lateral stability produces Dutch roll; too weak produces spiral instability
  • A yaw damper is the usual cure on swept-wing jets (Etkin 8.7)
📖 Explained for complete beginners
When you meet "directional stability" in maintenance literature think of the fin: any repair that changes its shape or area affects it.
PART 5/5
05

The oscillation modes: Boeing 747 data

  • Two longitudinal modes: phugoid (long period, lightly damped) and short period (heavily damped)
  • The eigenvalue λ = σ ± iω gives the period T = 2π/ω and damping ratio ζ = −σ/|λ|
  • The Lanchester approximation gives a phugoid period that depends on flight speed only

📖 Explained for complete beginners
A negative σ means the oscillation dies out. The phugoid has a tiny σ so it takes over three minutes to halve, while the short-period oscillation halves in under two seconds.
PART 5/5 · 04

Phugoid versus short period: how they differ

  • Phugoid: speed and pitch attitude change strongly while angle of attack and pitch rate barely change; energy trades between height and speed (Etkin 6.2)
  • Short period: angle of attack and pitch rate change quickly while airspeed barely changes
  • Lanchester: T = π√2·u₀/g = 106.9 s for u₀ = 773.98 ft/s, against the true 93.5 s; Etkin quotes 107 s and 93 s
  • EASA describes the same behaviour: diminishing oscillations with the nose alternately rising and lowering, until the aircraft settles at the balancing speed
📖 Explained for complete beginners
The phugoid period is simply proportional to speed: half the speed gives half the period, and in this approximation it depends on neither the aircraft nor the altitude.

📜 history

Lanchester and the name "phugoid"

Frederick Lanchester described this long-period oscillation in 1908 and named it the phugoid. Etkin notes that the name comes from a Greek root meaning "fugitive" while Lanchester actually wanted the root for "fly"; although not exact, the word has become standard jargon (Etkin p. 167). His solution assumed constant total energy, which gives a period that depends only on speed.

🔎 case

Case: checking the CG after loading

Suppose an aircraft has its neutral point at 35% MAC and the aft CG limit must stay at least 5% MAC ahead of the NP (Etkin 1.5), i.e. CG ≤ 30% MAC. A loading puts the CG at 25% MAC: the static margin K_n = 0.10, or 10% MAC, satisfies the requirement; if the CG slipped back to 32% MAC then K_n would be only 0.03 and insufficient. The numbers are illustrative; the formula K_n = h_n − h is Etkin 2.3.

⚠️ watch out

Common pitfalls

(1) Longitudinal stability is about the LATERAL axis and lateral stability about the LONGITUDINAL axis. (2) EASA numbers the answers 4-1 to 4-4 but the questions are 4-1, 4-2, 4-5, 4-6: answer 4-3 (dihedral) and 4-4 (yawing, directional) belong to questions 4-5 and 4-6; this module pairs them by content. (3) Positive static stability is not enough: an oscillation can be statically positive but dynamically negative (growing amplitude). (4) The phugoid is not a small-speed oscillation: speed and height change greatly while angle of attack does not. (5) The Lanchester approximation neglects drag, so it gives 107 s instead of the B747's 93 s.

✍️ Worked problems

Two Etkin end-of-chapter-6 exercises solved step by step with the B747 data printed in the book (Table 6.1, matrix A, eigenvalues 6.2,3). The answers are recomputed with numpy; they can be checked because the book prints the eigenvalues.

6.1. Use (6.3,10) to compute the approximate phugoid period T = 2π/ω_n for the B747 at u₀ = 773.98 ft/s, assuming M_u = 0.

Etkin & Reid 3rd, Exercise 6.1

👁️ Show the solution

With M_u = 0 the Lanchester approximation gives ω_n = g√2/u₀ so T = 2π/ω_n = π√2·u₀/g.

T = π√2 × 773.98/32.174 = 106.9 s.

Etkin quotes 107 s; the true period from the eigenvalues is 93.5 s (a 14% difference because the approximation neglects drag).

6.4. Prove that the coefficient of λ^(N−1) in an Nth-order characteristic polynomial is the negative of the sum of the real parts of all the roots; check it for the B747.

Etkin & Reid 3rd, Exercise 6.4

👁️ Show the solution

If the polynomial has roots λ₁…λ_N then p(λ) = Π(λ − λ_i); the coefficient of λ^(N−1) is −Σλ_i. Complex roots come in conjugate pairs so the imaginary parts cancel, leaving −ΣRe λ_i.

B747: roots −0.003289 ± 0.06723i and −0.3719 ± 0.8875i so −ΣRe = 2(0.003289 + 0.3719) = 0.750468, matching the 0.750468 of (6.2,2).
