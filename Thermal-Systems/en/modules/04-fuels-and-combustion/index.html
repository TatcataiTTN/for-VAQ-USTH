Part 1 · Foundations

A reacting system must count chemical energy as well, and the usual fuels are all hydrocarbons CₙHₘ

Slide Ch.4 p.3–4

  • A non-reacting system needs only internal energy (tied to temperature and pressure) and latent internal energy (phase change). A reacting system must also include chemical internal energy, tied to the destruction and formation of bonds between atoms; the energy balance relations already known still apply but must carry this term (p.3).
  • A fuel is any material that can be burned to release thermal energy; most consist of hydrogen and carbon and are called hydrocarbon fuels, written CnHm, and exist in all phases: coal, gasoline, natural gas (p.4).
  • Liquid fuels come from crude oil by distillation: the most volatile fraction is gasoline, then kerosene, diesel fuel and fuel oil (figure p.4).
One-substance models of real fuels Added from the book: Cengel §15-1; A-27

Liquid fuels are mixtures of many hydrocarbons but are usually treated as one substance: gasoline ≈ octane C₈H₁₈, diesel ≈ dodecane C₁₂H₂₆, natural gas ≈ methane CH₄ (Cengel p.752). Heating values of some fuels from Table A-27:

📖 Explanation for beginners
When water warms up only its temperature changes. When gasoline burns, the C–C and C–H bonds are broken, C=O and O–H bonds form, and the energy difference between the two sets of bonds is the heat of combustion. So the energy balance of a combustor needs an extra "item", chemical energy, that earlier chapters did not need. We lump every fuel into the formula CₙHₘ to keep the mole counting simple.
Part 1 · Foundations

Every kmol of O₂ in air comes with 3.76 kmol of N₂, and the inert N₂ absorbs most of the energy released

Slide Ch.4 p.5–7

Dry air (p.5–6)
$$\text{dry air}\approx 21\%\,\mathrm{O_2}+79\%\,\mathrm{N_2}\ \Rightarrow\ 1\,\mathrm{kmol\,O_2}+\frac{0.79}{0.21}\,\mathrm{kmol\,N_2}=1\,\mathrm{kmol\,O_2}+3.76\,\mathrm{kmol\,N_2}=4.76\,\mathrm{kmol\ air}$$
  • $\mathrm{N_2}$argon is counted as N₂; trace gases are disregarded
  • Real dry air (by volume) is 20.9 % O₂, 78.1 % N₂, 0.9 % Ar and traces (p.5); 21/79 is the convention of the chapter.
  • Pure oxygen is used only for welding and cutting (p.5); air is the standard oxidizer.
  • N₂ is inert: it does not react with other elements (apart from a very small amount of nitric oxides) but matters a lot, because it enters in large quantity at low temperature and leaves much hotter, absorbing a large share of the chemical energy (p.7).

In numbers: 3.76 = 0.79/0.21 = 3.762; 1 kmol of dry air has M_air = 28.97 kg/kmol (Table A-1; slide p.14), usually rounded to 29.

📖 Explanation for beginners
Air is nearly "four parts N₂ to one part O₂". To get 1 kmol of O₂ you must blow in 4.76 kmol of air, of which 3.76 kmol of N₂ merely "come along" and leave hot. That N₂ is what pulls the flame temperature down compared with burning in pure oxygen.
Part 1 · Foundations

Water vapor in the gases is inert but condenses below the dew point, and a fuel must be heated above its ignition temperature to burn

Slide Ch.4 p.8–10

  • The air entering a combustor normally carries some moisture. For most combustion processes the moisture of the air and the H₂O formed in combustion are treated as an inert gas, like N₂. At very high temperature some water vapor dissociates into H₂, O₂, H, O and OH (p.8).
  • When the gases cool below the dew point water vapor condenses; the droplets combine with any SO₂ to form highly corrosive sulfuric acid, so the dew point must be predicted (p.8).
  • Reactants exist before the reaction, products after it. A reactant need not react: burning C with air leaves N₂ on both sides (p.9).
  • Bringing fuel into contact with oxygen is not enough: the fuel must be above its ignition temperature and the mixture must be in the right range; natural gas does not burn in air below 5 percent or above about 15 percent (p.10).

Part 1 · Foundations

Balance a combustion equation element by element because atoms are conserved and moles are not; AF is on a mass basis

Slide Ch.4 p.11–12

  • Chemical equations are balanced by the conservation of mass: the total mass of each element is conserved and, since mass = number of atoms × atomic mass, the number of atoms of each element is conserved (p.11).
  • But the total number of moles is not conserved (p.11): in Example 15–1, 1 + 95.2 = 96.2 kmol go in and 8 + 9 + 7.5 + 75.2 = 99.7 kmol come out.
  • The air–fuel ratio AF is usually on a mass basis; mass is related to the moles by m = NM (M: molar mass) (p.12).
Air–fuel ratio (p.12, p.17)
$$\mathrm{AF}=\frac{m_{air}}{m_{fuel}}=\frac{(NM)_{air}}{(NM)_{fuel}},\qquad m=NM$$
  • $N$kmol
  • $M$molar mass [kg/kmol]; the slides use M_air = 29 (p.17), C = 12, H₂ = 2
  • $\mathrm{AF}$kg of air per kg of fuel; its inverse is the fuel–air ratio (FA)

The figure on p.12 draws H₂ + ½ O₂ → H₂O with the labels "2 kg hydrogen", "16 kg oxygen": 2 kg of H₂ combine with 16 kg of O₂ (each element's mass is conserved). The slide does not print "18 kg of water": that is inferred.

📖 Explanation for beginners
Like Lego: the number of bricks (atoms) is fixed but the number of "models" (molecules) can change. One octane molecule plus O₂ becomes many CO₂ and H₂O molecules, so the mole count changes while the number of C, H, O atoms does not. AF answers "how many kg of air burn 1 kg of fuel": multiply the moles by the molar mass to get kg.

Part 2 · Methods

Theoretical combustion uses exactly the O₂ the element balance needs and leaves no free O₂; hydrogen always burns out before carbon

Slide Ch.4 p.18–22

  • Complete combustion: all C burns to CO₂, all H to H₂O, all S (if any) to SO₂; it is incomplete if the products contain unburned fuel, C, H₂, CO or OH (p.18).
  • Causes of incomplete combustion (p.19): insufficient oxygen; insufficient mixing in the short contact time (even with excess oxygen); dissociation at high temperature.
  • Oxygen has a greater tendency to combine with hydrogen than with carbon: H always burns to H₂O while some C may end up as CO or soot (p.20).
  • The theoretical (stoichiometric) air, 100 %, is the smallest amount that burns the fuel completely: no free O₂ in the products (p.21). Example: CH₄ + 2(O₂ + 3.76 N₂) → CO₂ + 2 H₂O + 7.52 N₂ (p.22).
Theoretical O₂ for C_cH_hO_o (O₂ balance) Added from the book: El-Sayed Eq.10.8; the O term follows from the O₂ balance
$$a_{th}=c+\frac{h}{4}-\frac{o}{2},\qquad \mathrm{AF}_{st}=\frac{a_{th}\,(4.76)\,M_{air}}{M_{fuel}}$$

Part 2 · Methods

Real combustion cannot be predicted by mass balance alone so it is measured, and the Orsat gives the dry-basis composition

Slide Ch.4 p.25–26

  • Actual combustion is hardly ever complete, even with excess air, so the product composition cannot be predicted from the mass balance alone; the only alternative is to measure each component (p.25).
  • The Orsat analyzer (p.26): collect a sample of the gases, cool it to room temperature and pressure, measure its volume; contact it with a CO₂ absorber and measure again; the volume reduction over the original volume is the volume fraction of CO₂ = mole fraction (ideal gas). Repeat for the other gases.
Why is the Orsat composition on a dry basis? Added from the book: Cengel §15-2

The sample is collected over water and stays saturated, so the vapor pressure is constant during the test and the water vapor is ignored; the data are reported on a dry basis. The H₂O formed follows from the balanced equation (coefficient b in Example 15–4).

📖 Explanation for beginners
If you know the fuel and the air you can compute the products ("ideal" combustion). If you only know the measured products you work backwards: from the dry composition find the real combustion equation, then the AF. That is the "reverse combustion analysis" of Part 3.

Part 3 · Worked examples

The ethane example shows that the excess-air factor multiplies both O₂ and N₂ while only 0.2 a_th of O₂ is left over

Slide Ch.4 p.27–30

  • Problem (p.27): ethane C₂H₆ burns with 20 percent excess air, complete combustion, ideal gases, total pressure 100 kPa. Find (a) AF and (b) the dew point.
  • The products hold only CO₂, H₂O, N₂ and the excess O₂: C₂H₆ + 1.2 a_th(O₂ + 3.76 N₂) → 2 CO₂ + 3 H₂O + 0.2 a_th O₂ + (1.2 × 3.76) a_th N₂ (p.28). The factor 1.2 already accounts for the 20 % excess; N₂ is the same on both sides.
O₂ balance (p.29)
$$1.2\,a_{th}=2+1.5+0.2\,a_{th}\ \Rightarrow\ a_{th}=3.5\ \Rightarrow\ \mathrm{C_2H_6}+4.2(\mathrm{O_2}+3.76\,\mathrm{N_2})\to2\,\mathrm{CO_2}+3\,\mathrm{H_2O}+0.7\,\mathrm{O_2}+15.79\,\mathrm{N_2}$$

With M_air = 28.97 and M(C₂H₆) = 30.07 (A-27): AF = 19.31 (the slide shows 19.3; 19.33 with 29/30).

Writing "1.2 a_th" in place of the unknown is a trick to build the 20 % excess in from the start. After the O₂ balance you get a_th = 3.5 and every coefficient is known: 4.2 kmol of O₂, 15.79 kmol of N₂ and 0.7 kmol of left-over O₂.

When cooled below the dew point the remaining vapor must have a partial pressure equal to the saturation pressure, so most of the water condenses

Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

The enthalpy of every reactant and product must be counted from one common standard state, 25 °C and 1 atm

Slide Ch.4 p.40–44

  • A system has four forms of energy: sensible and latent (phase change), chemical (molecular structure) and nuclear (p.40). A reaction breaks and forms bonds so the chemical energy of reactants and products differ; that difference must appear in the energy balance (p.41).
  • Ignoring nuclear reactions and kinetic/potential energy: ΔE_sys = ΔE_state + ΔE_chem; when the products leave at the inlet state of the reactants, ΔE_state = 0 and only ΔE_chem remains (p.42).
  • A common standard reference state is needed: 25 °C (77 °F), 1 atm, marked by the superscript ° (p.43). Ideal-gas tables are used through the difference h̄(T) − h̄°; for N₂ at 500 K: 14,581 − 8669 = 5912 kJ/kmol (p.44, A-18).
Sensible enthalpy relative to the standard state (p.44)
$$\bar h_{500\,K}-\bar h^\circ=14{,}581-8669=5912\ \mathrm{kJ/kmol}\quad(\mathrm{N_2})$$

Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

The higher heating value exceeds the lower one by exactly the heat of vaporization of the water formed

Slide Ch.4 p.47–52

  • The enthalpy of reaction h_R is the enthalpy of the products minus that of the reactants at the same state for a complete reaction; for combustion it is the enthalpy of combustion h_C: the heat released when 1 kmol (or 1 kg) of fuel burns completely at a specified temperature and pressure (p.47). h_C of carbon = −393,520 kJ/kmol; h_C depends on temperature and pressure and is of little use for incomplete combustion, so h̄_f° is used (p.48).
  • Heating value = |h_C| [kJ/kg of fuel]: the heat released when the fuel burns completely and the products return to the reactants' state (p.51). Liquid water in the products gives the HHV, vapor the LHV (p.51); they are related through h_fg (p.52).
Enthalpy of combustion and the HHV–LHV relation (p.47, 52)

  • $m$mass of H₂O in the products per kg of fuel
  • $h_{fg}$enthalpy of vaporization of water at the stated temperature: 2,441.7 kJ/kg at 25 °C (A-4)

A gas-turbine combustor runs lean (φ < 1) to bring the flame temperature down to what the materials can withstand

Slide Ch.4 p.4 Slide Ch.4 p.55 Added from the book: El-Sayed §10.7 Ex.10.3; Farokhi §7.3; Cengel Ex.15-8

  • Aviation fuels are distillation fractions of crude oil (kerosene, p.4). When the composition varies with the source (coal, natural gas, oil) the heating value is usually measured in a bomb calorimeter at constant volume or in a steady-flow device instead of being computed from h̄_f° (p.55).
  • Gas-turbine combustors run lean (El-Sayed §10.7): to avoid an excessive temperature at the combustor exit the overall F/A must be much smaller than stoichiometric (φ < 1). Example 10.3: fuel C₁₂H₂₄ (the book wrongly calls it octane), Q_HV = 42,800 kJ/kg, T₀₁ = 650 K, T₀₂ = 1600 K, cold/hot c_p = 1.005/1.23: f = 0.0322; f_st = 0.0678 ⇒ φ = 0.475 (book 0.4749): a lean mixture.

📖 Explanation for beginners
Fuel burning near stoichiometric gives the hottest flame but turbine blades cannot stand it. For the same fuel, the leaner the mixture the cooler the flame, just as the dew-point figure shows: more excess air pulls every temperature down.

3. Theory context

Combustion is an exothermic oxidation; every calculation of the chapter rests on three familiar laws: conservation of atoms (balanced equation), the ideal-gas equation with Dalton's law (P_v = y_v P, dew point), and the first law (heat released is an enthalpy difference, computed through h̄_f°). Setting h̄_f° = 0 for stable elements at 25 °C, 1 atm turns every reaction into an additive balance: Hess's law in the form "h_C = Σ products − Σ reactants". Dissociation (slide p.8) and incomplete combustion lower the real temperature below the ideal value; computing them needs chemical-equilibrium constants (Farokhi §7.4, NOT DONE). No historical data are given because the sources read do not supply them.

5. Exam pitfalls

  • The coefficient before (O₂ + 3.76 N₂) is moles of O₂; moles of air = 4.76 × coefficient; AF = moles of air × 29 / fuel mass.
  • Dew point = T_sat(P_v), P_v = (N_v/N_prod)·P_prod: divide by the TOTAL product moles (H₂O included); A-5 interpolation is up to 0.4 °C off IAPWS.
  • Condensate: solve (N_v − N_w)/(N_prod − N_w) = P_sat/P, do not forget N_w in the denominator.
  • Reverse analysis: take 100 kmol of dry products; N₂ → a, C → x, H → b; use the O₂ balance to check, not to solve.
  • The enthalpy of combustion is negative (exothermic); HHV uses liquid H₂O, LHV vapor H₂O; HHV − LHV = m h_fg.
  • When asked for heat out, remember the sensible part h̄(T) − h̄(298 K) of the products (and of the entering air if not at 25 °C); convert °C to K.
  • Printed answers may use exact molar masses (Cengel 15–104: 29.41 / 1.77 appear only with 28.97, 44.097, 58.123) or rounded ones (15–119, 15–120: pick the nearest option); state the set used.
