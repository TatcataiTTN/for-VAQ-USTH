Part 1 · Foundations

Cascade amplifiers

  • One single-transistor stage gives a voltage gain of only about 10–100 (EASA)
  • Cascading gives large gain: the total gain is the PRODUCT of the stage gains
Overall gain
Av(total) = Av1 · Av2 · … · Avn ; dB(total) = dB1 + dB2 + … + dBn
📖 Explanation for beginners
In decibels multiplication becomes addition — handier for mental arithmetic.
Part 1 · Foundations

The four coupling methods

CouplingFeatureUsed in

📖 Explanation for beginners
Choose by whether the signal has a dc component and whether impedance matching is needed.

Positive and negative feedback

Feedback
Acl = A / (1 + A·B) → 1/B khi A·B ≫ 1
TypePhase of returned signalEffect
Positivein phaseraises amplitude; too much → oscillation
Negativeout of phaselowers amplitude but stabilises
📖 Explanation for beginners
Negative feedback trades gain for stability (Module 02).

Part 2 · Methods

Class B and AB push-pull

  • Two transistors carry the load peaks alternately: when one conducts the other is off
  • Class B: biased right at cutoff; crossover distortion
  • Class AB: small forward bias (diodes/current mirror) removes crossover distortion
Saturation current
Ic(sat) = VCC / RL ; η(max) = π/4 ≈ 78.5 %
📖 Explanation for beginners
A complementary npn + pnp pair is needed if no transformer is used.
Part 2 · Methods

RC coupling and low-frequency response

Corner frequency of RC coupling
fc = 1/(2π·R·C)
  • R: total resistance seen by the coupling capacitor (next stage input)
  • Signals well below fc are attenuated (the capacitor blocks)
📖 Explanation for beginners
For a lower fc raise C or the input resistance.
Part 2 · Methods

Loading between stages

  • The next stage loads the previous one: its Rin divides against the previous output impedance
  • Real overall gain = (divider factor) × Av1 × Av2
Example · Example
Av1 = 20, Av2 = 50, divider 0.9 → 0.9 × 20 × 50 = 900
📖 Explanation for beginners
Ignoring loading overestimates the gain.
Part 2 · Methods

Positive feedback and oscillation

Oscillation condition
A·B = 1 , φ = 0°
  • Oscillation needs positive feedback; EASA: direct output-to-input coupling gives maximum positive feedback but no amplitude control
  • Real designs add automatic bias control
📖 Explanation for beginners
See module 07 for Wien, phase-shift, Colpitts and the 555.

Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

Unit-conversion trap

Add dB, multiply ratiosAdding ratios or multiplying dB values is wrong.
📖 Explanation for beginners
20·log for voltage, 10·log for power.
Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

Drawbacks of each coupling

  • Direct coupling: supply and temperature drift shift the Q-point of the whole chain
  • RC coupling: capacitors limit low-frequency response and cannot pass dc
📖 Explanation for beginners
No coupling method is perfect.
Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

Limits of feedback

  • Too much positive feedback → oscillation and distortion; amplitude must be limited
  • Negative feedback lowers gain, so extra stages may be needed to compensate
📖 Explanation for beginners
Negative feedback trades gain for stability.

3. History and theory context

Negative feedback was invented by Harold Black in 1927 to stabilise long-distance telephone amplifiers; cascading and amplifier classes are the outcome of balancing gain, efficiency and distortion.

4. Aircraft case study (illustrative)

Illustrative: a push-pull power stage driving a servomotor (EASA SM03). Not a specific system.

5. Exam pitfalls

  • Adding gains instead of multiplying.
  • Using 10·log for voltage.
  • Mixing positive (in phase) and negative (out of phase) feedback.
  • Forgetting that class B conducts only 50 % of the cycle.
