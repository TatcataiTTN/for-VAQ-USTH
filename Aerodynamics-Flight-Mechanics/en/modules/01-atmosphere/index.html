MODULE 01 · Atmosphere

Physics of the Atmosphere and the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA)

Every aerodynamic force comes from the air, so the first thing to know is the state of that air: its pressure, density and temperature. This module covers those three quantities, how they change with height and the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA), then puts them to work in three concrete cases: a Boeing 727 wing at altitude, a Zeppelin airship and a high-altitude airport on a hot day.

EASA Module 08, Sub-module 01 (pp. 1.1–1.8) · Anderson ch.1 sec. 1.4 and Appendix D · Etkin & Reid Appendix D

PART 1/5
01

Air is a fluid

  • Air is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, and is a fluid because it can flow and take the shape of its container
  • Unlike water, air is compressible
  • Aerodynamics studies bodies moving through air and the forces produced
PART 1/5 · 01

What aerodynamics is and why it matters

  • "aero" means air and "dyne" means force. Aerodynamics studies objects moving through air and the forces that produce or change that motion
  • To maintain a modern aircraft properly you need to know why its surfaces must be aerodynamically smooth and why it has both primary and secondary controls
  • Aerodynamically, an aircraft is an object travelling through space and affected by changes in atmospheric conditions
In plain words
Hold your hand out of a moving car window: it is pushed even though it touches nothing solid. Air moving relative to a body puts a force on it, and this whole course is about describing that force.
PART 1/5 · 01

Three air properties: p, ρ, T

  • Pressure p (N/m² or Pa): force per unit area
  • Density ρ (kg/m³): mass per unit volume
  • Temperature T (kelvin): a measure of the thermal motion of the molecules
  • At near-standard conditions the three are linked by the perfect-gas equation of state p = ρRT with R = 287 J/(kg·K) (Anderson 1.1)
In plain words
Knowing two of the three gives the third. Remember to convert temperature to kelvin: K = °C + 273.15.
PART 2/5
02

Atmospheric pressure and its units

  • Atmospheric pressure is the weight of the air column above divided by the area
  • Standard sea level: 1 atm = 14.7 psi = 29.92 inHg = 1013.2 mb (hPa) = 760 mmHg
  • Pressure falls quickly with altitude
PART 2/5 · 01

P = F/A and the barometer

  • Force F = area A × pressure P, so P = F/A
  • A one-square-inch column of air from sea level to the top of the atmosphere weighs about 14.7 lb, so sea-level pressure is 14.7 psi
  • A barometer uses a mercury column and reads pressure in inches of mercury (inHg)
  • Meteorology now uses the hectopascal: 1 hPa = 100 Pa
In plain words
Pressure is force spread over an area. The same person sinks deeper in a stiletto heel than in a flat sole because the force is concentrated on a smaller area.

PART 2/5 · 01

Pressure decreases with altitude

  • The air column above is shorter and lighter, so pressure drops
  • At 40 000 ft pressure is only 2.72 psi (EASA Figure 1-4 also gives 2.72 psi), less than a fifth of the sea-level value
  • At 50 000 ft pressure is almost one tenth of sea level (EASA)
  • As an aircraft climbs, pressure, oxygen and temperature all drop, affecting lift and engine power
In plain words
Pressure falls by a constant factor, not a constant amount: it halves for roughly every 5.5 km of climb. So by 40 000 ft (about 12 km) more than 80% of it is gone.

PART 3/5
03

Density and humidity

  • Density is proportional to pressure and inversely proportional to temperature
  • Humid air is lighter than dry air
  • Low density reduces lift and thrust and lengthens the take-off run
PART 3/5 · 01

Two rules for density

  • Density varies directly with pressure
  • Density varies inversely with temperature
  • So high-altitude air is thinner than low-altitude air, and a hot air mass is thinner than a cold one
  • An aircraft can fly faster at high altitude because there are fewer air particles per unit volume, so less resistance
In plain words
Both rules follow from p = ρRT. At constant T, density rises with p; at constant p, density falls as T rises.
PART 3/5 · 01

Humidity makes air lighter

  • Absolute humidity: mass of water vapour per unit volume of air
  • Relative humidity: the percentage ratio of the water vapour present to the amount at saturation at the same temperature and pressure
  • Water vapour weighs about 5/8 as much as the same volume of dry air, so humid air has lower density
  • On damp days an aircraft needs a longer take-off runway than on dry days
In plain words
A muggy day feels "heavy", so it is natural to think humid air weighs more. It is the other way round: a water molecule (mass 18) takes the place of a nitrogen or oxygen molecule (28 or 32), so the mixture gets lighter.
PART 4/5
04

Temperature and the atmospheric layers

  • Troposphere: temperature falls about 2 °C per 1000 ft
  • The tropopause is about −57 °C
  • Most civil aviation takes place in the troposphere
PART 4/5 · 01

Troposphere and tropopause

  • On average the troposphere reaches about 38 000 ft; over the poles only 25 000–30 000 ft, at the equator about 60 000 ft
  • Temperature falls almost steadily, about −2 °C or −3.5 °F per 1000 ft
  • The upper boundary is the tropopause, a zone of nearly constant −57 °C (−69 °F)
In plain words
−2 °C per 1000 ft is about −6.5 °C per km, which is the 0.0065 K/m gradient the ISA uses on the next slides.
PART 4/5 · 01

The layers above

  • Stratosphere: temperature rises with altitude to near 0 °C; it holds the ozone layer that blocks ultraviolet rays
  • Mesosphere: temperature falls again
  • The thermosphere lies above
  • Some civil and many military flights take place in the stratosphere
In plain words
Temperature does not fall forever with height: in the stratosphere ozone absorbs ultraviolet and the air warms. The ISA model, however, holds temperature constant from 11 to 20 km, so the ISA table does not show this warming.
PART 5/5
05

The International Standard Atmosphere (ISA)

  • The real atmosphere always changes, so a standard model is needed for comparison and calibration
  • ISA at sea level: 15 °C, 1013.25 hPa, density 1.225 kg/m³
  • Agreed and used by ICAO, ISO and governments

PART 5/5 · 01

Application: density altitude

  • ISA underlies the altimeter, aircraft performance figures and comparisons between manufacturers
  • Example: an airport at 5000 ft and 30 °C (about 25 °C above ISA). The real density, 0.969 kg/m³, is only 79% of standard sea level, which corresponds to a density altitude of about 7801 ft
  • That is why take-off performance suffers at high airports on hot days
In plain words
Density altitude is the height in the ISA at which the air has the same density as the air actually around you. The aircraft performs as if it were at that height.

History

From Torricelli's tube to ISA

In 1643 Evangelista Torricelli built the first mercury barometer and showed that air has weight. In 1648, at Blaise Pascal's request, Florin Périer carried a barometer up the Puy de Dôme and saw the mercury column fall as he climbed: pressure drops with altitude. Later ICAO, ISO and governments agreed on one standard atmosphere, so that altimeters and aircraft performance tables share a common baseline (EASA, p. 1.5).

Case

Case: a Zeppelin and a Boeing 727 wing

  • Anderson 1.1, Boeing 727 wing. At a point on the wing p = 1.9×10⁴ N/m² and T = 203 K, so ρ = p/RT = 0.326 kg/m³, about a third of sea-level density.
  • Anderson 1.12, Zeppelin. A 15 000 m³ airship flies at 1000 m (ISA ρ = 1.1116 kg/m³). Archimedes buoyancy is 1.634×10⁵ N and aerodynamic lift is 3850 N, so the total weight is 1.673×10⁵ N. Buoyancy carries almost all of it; aerodynamic lift is only 2.3%.
Pitfalls

Common pitfalls

  • Humid air is lighter than dry air, not heavier.
  • Pressure does not fall evenly with height but roughly exponentially: at 40 000 ft only about 18% of the sea-level value is left.
  • The −2 °C per 1000 ft gradient holds only in the troposphere. Above the tropopause the ISA temperature is constant.
  • Anderson 1.12 includes Archimedes buoyancy. Forget it and the weight is off by a factor of dozens.
  • The Solution Manual (4th ed., checked against the page image, p. 12) prints L = 3487 N on the lift line, but 500 × 153.9 × 0.05 = 3847 N, and the very next line uses 3847 N to reach W = 1.67×10⁵ N. The final answer is right; only the middle line has a typo.

Worked problems

Three end-of-chapter 1 problems from Anderson, solved step by step. The problems come from the 6th edition; each solution was recomputed and compared with the Solution Manual (4th edition, handwritten).

1.1. (a) At a point on a Boeing 727 wing, p = 1.9×10⁴ N/m² and T = 203 K. Find ρ. (b) In the test section of a supersonic wind tunnel, p = 1058 lb/ft² and ρ = 1.23×10⁻³ slug/ft³. Find T.

Anderson 6th, Problem 1.1

Show the solution

What the problem says. Two of the three air properties p, ρ, T are given and the third is asked: (a) find ρ, (b) find T.

Idea. Both parts use the equation of state p = ρRT. Rearrange it for the unknown and use the value of R that matches the unit system.

Steps.

  1. (a) The data are in SI units, so R = 287 J/(kg·K) and ρ = p/(RT).
  2. Substituting: ρ = 1.9×10⁴/(287 × 203) = 1.9×10⁴/58 261 = 0.326 kg/m³.
  3. (b) The data are in English units, so R = 1716 ft·lb/(slug·°R) and T = p/(ρR).
  4. Substituting: T = 1058/(1.23×10⁻³ × 1716) = 1058/2.1107 = 501 °R.

Check: The Solution Manual gives 0.326 kg/m³ and 501 °R. Both are plausible: 501 °R is about 41 °F or 278 K, a normal wind-tunnel temperature, and 0.326 kg/m³ is about a third of sea-level density, as expected at altitude.

1.11. A mercury U-tube manometer connects a wind-tunnel model to the atmosphere (p_atm = 1.01×10⁵ N/m²). The two mercury levels differ by 20 cm, with the higher level on the model side. Find the pressure on the model wing.

Anderson 6th, Problem 1.11

Show the solution

What the problem says. A mercury U-tube manometer: one leg on the model, the other open to the atmosphere; the mercury levels differ by 20 cm with the higher level on the model side. Find the pressure on the model.

Idea. Mercury is pushed up on the lower-pressure side. So the model side is below atmospheric by ρ_Hg g h.

Steps.

  1. The higher level is on the model side, so p_model = p_atm − ρ_Hg g h.
  2. ρ_Hg g h = 1.36×10⁴ × 9.8 × 0.2 = 26 656 N/m².
  3. p = 1.01×10⁵ − 26 656 = 74 344 N/m² ≈ 7.43×10⁴ N/m².

Check: The Solution Manual gives 7.43×10⁴ N/m². Adding instead of subtracting would give 1.28×10⁵ N/m², above atmospheric; the mercury would then be lower on the model side, contrary to the problem.

1.12. A Zeppelin of volume 15 000 m³ and maximum diameter 14 m flies straight and level at V = 30 m/s at a standard altitude of 1000 m, with C_L = 0.05 (based on maximum cross-section). Find the total weight.

Anderson 6th, Problem 1.12

Show the solution

What the problem says. A Zeppelin of volume 15 000 m³ and maximum diameter 14 m flies straight and level at V = 30 m/s at a standard 1000 m, with C_L = 0.05 based on the maximum cross-section. Find its total weight.

Idea. Level flight means the total upward force equals the weight. Here that force has two parts: Archimedes buoyancy B (from the airship's volume) and aerodynamic lift L (from moving through the air).

Steps.

  1. The ISA density at 1000 m is ρ = 1.11164 kg/m³ (Anderson's table gives 1.1117; the difference is rounding in the last digit).
  2. B = ρVg = 1.11164 × 15 000 × 9.8 ≈ 1.634×10⁵ N.
  3. q = ½ρV² = ½ × 1.11164 × 30² = 500.2 Pa; S = πd²/4 = 153.9 m².
  4. L = qSC_L = 500.2 × 153.9 × 0.05 = 3850 N.
  5. W = B + L ≈ 1.634×10⁵ + 3850 ≈ 1.67×10⁵ N.

Check: Buoyancy carries 97.7% of the weight, so an answer of order 10⁵ N is plausible. The Solution Manual misprints L = 3487 N on the middle line (it should be 3847 N), but its final 1.67×10⁵ N is right.
