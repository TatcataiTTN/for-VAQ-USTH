Part 1 · Foundations

The binary system

  • Electricity has two states ON/OFF, so it represents the binary digits 1/0
  • Past 1 the column resets to 0 and a 1 carries to the next column on the left
  • Decimal → binary: take the largest column ≤ the number, put 1, subtract, repeat
Example · Example
13 = 8 + 4 + 1 = 1101₂ ; 1011₂ = 8 + 2 + 1 = 11
📖 Explanation for beginners
Each column is worth 1, 2, 4, 8, 16…; add the columns holding 1 to get the decimal value.

Part 2 · Methods

Combinational circuits: encoders, decoders, comparators

  • Encoder: decimal to binary/BCD; a priority encoder chooses when several inputs are HIGH
  • Decoder: enable LOW → all outputs LOW; enable HIGH → normal operation
  • Comparator: compares sets of binary numbers (parity checking)
📖 Explanation for beginners
The output depends only on the present inputs; there is no memory.

Part 2 · Methods

How ICs are made

  • Chips are made on thin semiconductor wafers; circuits are etched by photolithography and chemical steps; wafers are polished, cleaned, inspected
  • Chips are cut out, mounted in plastic/ceramic packages and wired to the pins
📖 Explanation for beginners
More transistors give higher speed but also more power and heat.
Part 2 · Methods

Op-amps in the logic context

  • Comparator op-amp: compares the signal with a reference and switches on/off
  • Voltage follower: gain 1, high input impedance
  • Integrator/differentiator: capacitor and resistor swap places (see module 03)
📖 Explanation for beginners
The comparator is the bridge between analog signals and logic levels.

Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

RS versus JK

The RS forbidden stateThe RS has a forbidden input combination; the JK fixes it with toggling at J = K = 1.
📖 Explanation for beginners
Do not confuse “toggle” (JK 1-1) with “forbidden” (RS 1-1).
Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

Easily confused gates

  • XOR excludes the 1-1 case, OR does not
  • NOR = OR then NOT; Negative AND ≡ NOR (De Morgan)
📖 Explanation for beginners
Write the truth table whenever in doubt.
Part 4 · Pitfalls and extensions

The enable pin and ESD

  • The enable pin performs no logic operation: it only activates or disables the decoder
  • CMOS uses little power but is ESD-sensitive (see module 09)

3. History and theory context

Boolean algebra (1854) lets circuits be described by logic; transistors and then integrated circuits (1960s) made gates small and cheap, paving the way for computers on aircraft.

4. Aircraft case study (illustrative)

Illustrative: a comparator turns a sensor signal into a logic level for a computer; not a specific system.

5. Exam pitfalls

  • Mixing XOR and OR in the 1-1 case.
  • Confusing NAND/NOR with AND/OR.
  • Forgetting the carry in binary addition.
  • Mixing TTL (+5 V) with CMOS (+3…+18 V).
